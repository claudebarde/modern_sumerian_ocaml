# Strip 1 — "Kakugmen"

Three balloons, eight signs, and already two different forms of the verb "to be".
This page walks through every word in the strip. You don't need to have read
anything else first.

> **How to read the boxes**
>
> Each balloon gets a card with the cuneiform, the transliteration and the
> translation. Underneath, every meaningful piece of the sentence — every
> *morpheme* — is explained in its own box.
>
> We write Sumerian using the Modern Sumerian conventions: **sh** for *š*, **h**
> for *ḫ*, **ĝ** for *ŋ*.

---

## Panel 1

> ## 𒅗𒆬𒈨𒂗
> ### Kakugmen
> **Transliteration** ka-kug-me-en
> **Translation** "I am Kakug."

| Sign | 𒅗 | 𒆬 | 𒈨 | 𒂗 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name | KA | KU₃ | ME | EN |
| Reads | ka | kug | me | en |

### Breaking it apart

```
ka.kug =Ø   =men
PN     =ABS =be:1SG.S
```

> **`ka-kug` — the name**
>
> Our character's name is an attested Sumerian personal name, and like most
> Sumerian names it means something: **"pure mouth"**, from `ka` "mouth" and
> `kug` "pure, shining" — the same word that gives "silver".
>
> Sumerian names are transparent little sentences and phrases of this kind:
> *Ur-Namma* "the one of (the goddess) Namma", *Lu-dingira* "man of the god",
> *Nin-kugzu* "the lady is wise". When you learn a name, you usually learn two or
> three ordinary words with it.

> **`-me-en` — "I am"**
>
> This is the whole of "I am", packed into one ending glued to the name.
>
> Sumerian has exactly one verb meaning "to be": **`me`**. It comes in two
> shapes. Sometimes it stands as a full verb with prefixes of its own — the
> *independent* copula. Sometimes it shrinks to an ending that clings to the end
> of the clause — the *enclitic* copula. The two are not free alternatives:
> together they fill a single set of forms, each covering the gaps the other
> leaves. For "I am", only the enclitic form exists. A separate word meaning "I
> am" simply never occurs.
>
> So `ka-kug-me-en` is *name + "am"*, and that is a complete sentence.

> **What isn't there: the word "I"**
>
> There's no pronoun in this sentence. The `-me-en` ending already says who the
> subject is, so adding `ĝe₂₆-e` "I" would be like saying "me, I am Kakug" —
> possible in Sumerian, but emphatic, and not what a person would normally say.
>
> The same pattern is attested with ordinary nouns: `lugal-me-en` "I am king",
> `dub-sar-me-en` "I am a scribe".

> **One for later: `-me-en` is also "you are"**
>
> Sumerian uses the very same ending for the first and second person singular.
> `lugal-me-en` can mean "I am king" *or* "you are king", and only context tells
> you which. This feels alarming at first and turns out to matter very little in
> practice — rather like English *you*, which is both singular and plural.

## Panel 2

> ## 𒌨𒂠𒈬
> ### Urgirĝu
> **Transliteration** ur-gir₁₅-ĝu₁₀
> **Translation** "My dog."

| Sign | 𒌨 | 𒂠 | 𒈬 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name | UR | EŠ₂ | MU |
| Reads | ur | gir₁₅ | ĝu₁₀ |

### Breaking it apart

```
ur.gir =ĝu  =Ø
dog    =my  =ABS
```

> **`ur-gir₁₅` — "dog"**
>
> Two signs, one word. `ur` on its own is a broad term covering dogs, lions and
> carnivores generally — closer to "beast" than to "dog". To say *dog*
> specifically, Sumerian uses the compound `ur-gir₁₅`.
>
> The second element is generally identified with `gir₁₅` "native, noble" — so
> the dog is, roughly, "the native beast", the one that belongs to the household
> rather than the wild. You will meet that same element again in a word you might
> not expect: **`eme-gir₁₅`**, "the native tongue", which is what the Sumerians
> called their own language.

> **`-ĝu₁₀` — "my"**
>
> Possession is a suffix, not a separate word. Sumerian says *dog-my*, in that
> order, and the suffix attaches to the end of the whole noun phrase — which is
> why it lands after `gir₁₅` rather than in the middle of the compound.
>
> This is the first sign of something that shapes the entire language: Sumerian
> is **agglutinative**. Meaning is added by stacking endings in a fixed order,
> each one doing one job. You will watch that stack grow over the next few
> strips.

> **What isn't there: a verb**
>
> Panel 2 has no "is" in it, because it isn't a sentence — it's a phrase. Kakug
> is pointing and labelling, the way you might say "my dog" while gesturing at
> one. Sumerian is quite comfortable with this, and you will find whole clauses
> in real texts with no copula at all.

> **Why the little numbers?**
>
> `gir₁₅`, `ĝu₁₀` — the subscript numbers are **not pronounced** and are not part
> of the word. Cuneiform had many different signs that could be read the same
> way, so scholars number them: `gir` is the reading of one sign, `gir₂` of
> another, `gir₁₅` of a third. The number tells you *which sign is on the tablet*.
> Read `gir₁₅` simply as "gir".

## Panel 3

> ## 𒃻𒈬𒌝
> ### Nindaĝum
> **Transliteration** ninda-ĝu₁₀-um
> **Translation** "That's my bread!"

| Sign | 𒃻 | 𒈬 | 𒌝 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name | GAR | MU | UM |
| Reads | ninda | ĝu₁₀ | um |

### Breaking it apart

```
ninda =ĝu  =Ø   =am
bread =my  =ABS =be:3SG.S
```

> **`ninda` — "bread"**
>
> One of the commonest words in the whole corpus — tens of thousands of
> attestations, most of them in ration lists and accounts. Bread and beer are the
> two poles of the Sumerian diet, and both will keep turning up in these strips.

> **`-ĝu₁₀` — "my", again**
>
> The same suffix as in panel 2, doing the same job. Note that it sits in the
> same place: straight after the noun.

> **`-um` — "it is"**
>
> Here is the verb "to be" once more, but in the third person this time: not "I
> am" but "**it is**".
>
> Its basic form is `-am`. When it attaches to something ending in the vowel
> */u/*, the two vowels contract and the result is written **`-um`**. So:
>
> `ninda` + `ĝu` + `am` → **nindaĝum**
>
> This is a real, attested spelling, and the same contraction shows up elsewhere:
> `nin₉-ĝu₁₀-um` "she is my sister", from a tablet from Umma written in the 21st
> century BCE.

> **The shape of the whole strip**
>
> Look at what the three balloons do together. Panel 1 gives you the copula in
> the first person, `-me-en`. Panel 2 gives you the possessive, `-ĝu₁₀`. Panel 3
> combines them and adds the copula in the third person, `-am`, hidden inside the
> contraction `-um`.
>
> Three balloons, and you have met both halves of the Sumerian verb "to be" and
> the first of the possessive suffixes.

## What this strip teaches

| Form | Meaning | Where |
|---|---|---|
| `-me-en` | "I am" (also "you are") — enclitic copula, 1st/2nd person singular | Panel 1 |
| `-ĝu₁₀` | "my" — 1st person singular possessive | Panels 2, 3 |
| `-am` → `-um` | "it is" — enclitic copula, 3rd person | Panel 3 |

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `ka` | mouth |
| `kug` | pure, shining; silver |
| `ur-gir₁₅` | dog |
| `ninda` | bread |

## Try it yourself

Using only what is on this page, how would you say:

1. "I am a scribe." (`dub-sar` = scribe)
2. "My bread."
3. "It is my dog."

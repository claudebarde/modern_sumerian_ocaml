# Strip 2 — "Kasham"

Last week's strip ended on **Nindaĝum**, "that's my bread", with the verb "to be"
buried inside a contraction where you couldn't quite see it. This week it steps
out into the open — and then does something English speakers rarely expect.

> **How to read the boxes**
>
> Each balloon gets a card with the cuneiform, the transliteration and the
> translation. Underneath, every meaningful piece of the sentence is explained in
> its own box.
>
> Modern Sumerian conventions: **sh** for *š*, **h** for *ḫ*, **ĝ** for *ŋ*.

---

## Panel 1

> ## 𒁉𒀀𒀭
> ### Kasham!
> **Transliteration** kaš-am₃
> **Translation** "It's beer!"

| Sign | 𒁉 | 𒀀𒀭 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | BI | A·AN |
| Reads | kaš | am₃ |

### Breaking it apart

```
kaš  =Ø   =am
beer =ABS =be:3SG.S
```

> **`kaš` — "beer"**
>
> Among the most frequent words in the entire corpus, with tens of thousands of
> attestations. Beer was not a luxury in Mesopotamia; it was a daily ration,
> issued by volume alongside bread, thick and nourishing and drunk by everyone.
>
> It is written with a single sign, 𒁉. That same sign can also be read `bi` or
> `ne` in other words — one sign, several possible readings, and context decides.
> This is normal in cuneiform and less confusing in practice than it sounds.

> **`-am₃` — "it is"**
>
> You have met this before, though you may not realise it. This is the third
> person of the verb `me` "to be" — the *enclitic copula*, the ending that clings
> to the end of the clause.
>
> In strip 1 it appeared as **`-um`**, in `ninda-ĝu₁₀-um`. Here it appears as
> **`-am₃`**. Same morpheme, different surroundings: `ninda-ĝu₁₀` ends in the
> vowel */u/*, and the two vowels contracted. `kaš` ends in a consonant, so
> nothing contracts, and you see the ending in its plain form.
>
> That is the small revelation this panel is built around. `-um` was `-am` all
> along.

> **What isn't there: a word for "it"**
>
> There is no subject in this sentence — no "it", no "this". The `-am₃` ending
> carries that information by itself. Sumerian only adds a separate subject when
> there is something worth specifying.

> **For the curious: why two signs for one syllable?**
>
> `am₃` is written 𒀀𒀭, the sign A followed by the sign AN. In the oldest texts,
> before about 2350 BCE, it was written with AN alone. The two-sign spelling
> arrived later for a reason that is audible rather than visual: the copula
> originally began with a glottal stop, and the older form was pronounced roughly
> *a'am*. When that glottal stop was lost, the vowels ran together into a single
> long vowel, and the scribes adopted the A·AN spelling for it.

## Panel 2

The middle panel is silent. Kakug lifts the jar and drinks, and there is nothing

## Panel 3, first balloon

> ## 𒁉𒉡
> ### Kash nu
> **Transliteration** kaš nu
> **Translation** "Not beer."

| Sign | 𒁉 | 𒉡 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | BI | NU |
| Reads | kaš | nu |

### Breaking it apart

```
kaš  =Ø   nu
beer =ABS NEG
```

> **`nu` — "not"**
>
> Here is the thing that surprises everyone. To say "it is beer", Sumerian adds
> the copula: `kaš-am₃`. To say "it is **not** beer", it does not add a negative
> copula — it takes the copula away and puts `nu` in its place.
>
> There is no such word as *`kaš nu-um`* or *`kaš nu-me`*. Those forms do not
> exist, and not because of some accident of preservation: the paradigm simply
> has a hole in it where the third-person negative ought to be. What fills the
> hole is a sentence with no verb at all.
>
> So `kaš nu` is not shorthand, and nothing has been left out. It is the complete
> and correct way to say "it is not beer".

> **Where your instinct *is* right**
>
> The gap is only in the third person. In the first and second person, the
> negative works exactly the way you would expect: `nu-me-en` "I am not". If
> Kakug wanted to deny being Kakug, he would need that form. It is only "he, she,
> it is not" that has to be built the other way.

> **For the curious: Kakug is quoting a king**
>
> Around 2100 BCE, the ruler Gudea of Lagash had an inscription carved on a
> statue of himself, explaining what the statue was made of. Within a few lines
> it uses both constructions:
>
> *"It is not silver, it is not lapis lazuli … it is not copper, not tin, not
> bronze … it is diorite."*
>
> Each denial is a bare noun plus `nu`. The final positive statement takes the
> copula: `esi-am₃`, "it is diorite". Deny, deny, then supply the right answer —
> which is exactly the shape of this strip, applied to a jar of water.

## Panel 3, second balloon

> ## 𒀀𒀀𒀭
> ### Aam
> **Transliteration** a-am₃
> **Translation** "It's water."

| Sign | 𒀀 | 𒀀𒀭 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | A | A·AN |
| Reads | a | am₃ |

### Breaking it apart

```
a     =Ø   =am
water =ABS =be:3SG.S
```

> **`a` — "water"**
>
> One of the shortest words in Sumerian: a single vowel, written with a single
> sign, 𒀀. It covers water in all its forms — drinking water, canal water, flood
> water, rain.

> **`-am₃` — "it is", once more**
>
> The same ending as panel 1, and here you can watch it behave differently again.
> `a` ends in a vowel, so the vowel of the copula contracts with it and the result
> is a single **long** vowel, /ām/.
>
> We write it **Aam**, with the vowel doubled. That is not a modern invention:
> Sumerian scribes did the same thing, writing an extra vowel sign to mark a long
> vowel — a practice that appears precisely where a contraction would predict
> one.

> **The two balloons together**
>
> Read them side by side and you have the pattern of the whole lesson:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | `kaš-am₃` | it **is** beer |
> | `kaš nu` | it is **not** beer |
> | `a-am₃` | it **is** water |
>
> One noun, one ending — or one noun and `nu`. That is the entire grammar of
> saying what something is, and is not, in Sumerian.

## What this strip teaches

| Form | Meaning | Where |
|---|---|---|
| `-am₃` | "it is" — enclitic copula, 3rd person, after a consonant | Panels 1, 3 |
| `nu` | "not" — negates a clause of this kind, and replaces the copula entirely | Panel 3 |
| `a-am₃` | the same copula after a vowel, contracting to a long vowel | Panel 3 |

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `kaš` | beer |
| `a` | water |

And one thing carried over from strip 1: the `-um` of **Nindaĝum** and the
`-am₃` of **Kasham** are the same ending.

## Try it yourself

Using what is on this page and in the notes to strip 1:

1. "It is bread." (`ninda` = bread)
2. "It is not water."
3. "It is my beer."

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. `ninda-am₃` — Nindam
2. `a nu` — A nu
3. `kaš-ĝu₁₀-um` — Kashĝum (the possessive `-ĝu₁₀` from strip 1, then the copula,
   contracting after */u/* exactly as in Nindaĝum)

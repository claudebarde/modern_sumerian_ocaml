# Expressing plural

> **A note on spelling.** This page uses the Modern Sumerian writing conventions:
> **š → sh**, **ḫ → h**, **ŋ → ĝ**. So _diŋir_ appears here as **diĝir**, _šabra_ as
> **shabra**, _ḫi-a_ as **hi-a**. Examples quoted from the reference grammars have been
> re-spelled to match, and their transliterations normalised to the index-number style
> (_é_ → **e₂**, _ĝír_ → **ĝir₂**).

---

## 1. Start by unlearning something

In English, plural is nearly compulsory. You cannot say _three house_ or _the god built
their temple_ without the grammar objecting. Every countable noun has two forms and you must
pick one.

Sumerian is not like this. Its starting position is:

> **Nouns are not inflected for number at all.**
> — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.1

The word **e₂** means 'house' _or_ 'houses'. **udu** means 'sheep' (one) _or_ 'sheep' (a
flock). Nothing in the word tells you, and usually nothing else does either. Sumerian simply
does not consider the question worth answering most of the time.

That is the baseline. Everything on this page is an _exception_ to it — a set of optional
tools Sumerian reaches for when the number genuinely matters. And they turn out to be
surprisingly varied: a plural word, reduplication, a special verb stem, agreement suffixes,
and a couple of late imports. Some mark nouns. Some mark verbs. Two of them are not really
"plural" at all in the English sense.

The page has two parts. **Part One** covers the noun; **Part Two** covers the verb, where
Sumerian does something that European languages simply do not do.

> **How to read the glossed examples on this page.** Line 1 is the transliteration as written
> on the tablet. Line 2 is the morphemic\* analysis, with `=` marking a clitic boundary and
> `-` an affix boundary. Line 3 labels each block; `ø` is an element present in the grammar
> but written with no sign. Square brackets show where each noun phrase begins and ends. The
> translation is always underneath.

# PART ONE — Plurality in the noun

## 2. The gender split: only humans get a plural marker

Sumerian sorts every noun into two **gender classes\***: **human** (people and gods) and
**non-human** (everything else — animals, objects, places, abstractions). One of the three
places this split shows up is number:

> "Only the human gender class has a number distinction between singular and plural. The
> enclitic plural marker {enē} is only used with human nouns."
> — Jagersma 2010: §6.2

So:

|                                                       | plural marking          |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| **diĝir** 'god', **dumu** 'child', **engar** 'farmer' | can take **=enē**       |
| **e₂** 'house', **udu** 'sheep', **kur** 'mountain'   | normally nothing at all |

If you are composing Sumerian, this is the single most useful rule on the page. _Do not_ try
to pluralise a non-human noun with `=enē`. There is no such construction.

## 3. The plural marker =/enē/

### It attaches to the phrase, not the noun

Like the case markers, `=enē` is a **phrase-final clitic\***. It goes on the _last word of
the noun phrase_, which may be a long way from the noun whose plurality it marks:

```
e₂   diĝir-re-ne-ke₄
[e₂  [diĝir=enē=ak ]=e ]
[house[god  =PL =GEN]=ERG]

'the temples of the gods'
MVN 15:138 7 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.1 ex. 12
```

```
diĝir gal-gal lagashki-a-ke₄-ne
[diĝir gal-gal [lagash=ak ]=enē=e ]
[god   big-big [Lagash=GEN]=PL =ERG]

'the great gods of Lagash'
Gudea Cylinder A 10:28 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.1 ex. 14
```

In that second example, `=enē` sits four words away from _diĝir_, the noun it actually
pluralises. Everything in between — the reduplicated adjective, the genitive phrase — is
modifying _diĝir_, so the plural marker has to wait until the phrase is finished.

It can even attach to a whole relative clause:

```
mu aga₃-us₂ urim₅ki-ta ma₂ lugal gid₂-da-ne-she₃
[mu [aga.us [urim=ta [ma lugal=ak]=ø gid-ø-'a]]=enē=ak]=she

'because of the guardsmen who towed the royal bark from Ur'
MVN 10:142 obv 19 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.3 ex. 27
```

### The slot order

When several clitics pile up on the same word, the order never varies:

> **possessive pronoun → plural marker {enē} → case marker**

```
ka   dumu-ne-ne-ka
[ka.g [dumu=ane=enē=ak ]='a ]
[mouth[child=her =PL =GEN]=LOC]

'in the mouth of her children'
NG 171 5 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.1 ex. 15
```

In Zólyomi's five-slot nominal template (2017: §3.1), `=enē` occupies **P4**, between the
possessor (P3) and the case marker (P5).

### What it looks like on the tablet

The underlying form is roughly **/enē/**, but you will rarely see it spelled that way,
because its initial /e/ contracts with a preceding vowel. Learn to recognise all of these:

| Written                | Underlying                                | Example                                                        |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **-e-ne**              | after a consonant                         | _engar-e-ne_ 'the farmers'                                     |
| **-ne**                | after a vowel                             | _geme₂-ne_ 'the slave women'; _shabra-ne_ 'the administrators' |
| **-re-ne**, **-le-ne** | with the preceding consonant carried over | _diĝir-re-ne_ 'the gods'                                       |
| **-a-ne**              | after /a/                                 | _dab₅-ba-ne_ 'the ones holding (a field)'                      |
| **-ne-ne**             | after a possessive _-ane_                 | _dumu-ne-ne_ 'her children'                                    |

(Jagersma 2010: §6.3.2, exx. 16–23)

A small piece of evidence worth savouring: the spelling _en-en-**ne₂-ne**-she₃_ 'for the
lords' (DP 77 4:1; Lagash, 24th c. BCE) uses **two different signs** for what sounds like the
same syllable — _ne₂_ for a short /nĕ/ and _ne_ for a long /nē/. That is how we know the
first vowel of `{enē}` is short and the second long.

## 4. When Sumerian _doesn't_ bother

This is the part that trips learners up, because the plural marker turns out to be optional
far more often than not.

> "Even with human nouns, explicit indication of the plural is relatively infrequent in
> Sumerian as compared with languages such as English or Akkadian. The plural marker {enē}
> is absent from human nouns if their plural meaning is already made clear by other means."
> — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.3

The logic is economical: **if you can already tell, don't mark it.** Four situations:

### With a numeral — never

```
2 sipa udu siki-ka
2 sipa [udu siki=ak]=ak
2 shepherd sheep wool=GEN=GEN

'two shepherds of wool sheep'   (not *sipa-ne)
VS 14:187 3:6 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.3 ex. 35
```

```
a-nun-na eridukiki ninnu-be₂       'the fifty Anunna-gods of Eridu'   (TrD 1 4)
shuku lu₂ 3-a-kam                  'It is the subsistence land of three persons.'   (NG 215 1)
```

The numeral has already said it. Adding `=enē` would be redundant.

### In the absolutive case — usually not

Attinger (1993: 160) noticed this and Jagersma confirms it: a noun in the absolutive
(the direct object, or the subject of an intransitive verb) normally lacks `=enē`, **because
the verb is already carrying the information**:

```
di-kud   ib-durunₓ(KU.KU)-ne₂-esh
dikud=ø  'i-b(i)-durun-esh
judge=ABS VP-3N:on-sit:PLUR-3PL.S

'The judges have sat there.'
TCS 1:203 3 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.3 ex. 39
```

Here _di-kud_ 'judge' is bare. But the verb uses the **plural stem** _durun_ and the
**plural suffix** _-esh_. Two signals is plenty; a third would be waste.

```
engar-me-esh
engar=ø=me-esh
farmer=ABS=be-3PL.S
'They are farmers.'   (BPOA 2:2562 rev 2; Umma, 21st c. BCE)
```

Zólyomi adds the same observation as a rule: the plural marker "is not used with human head
nouns modified with numerals or functioning as the subject of a copular clause"
(2017: §3.1).

### With collective groups — treated as non-human

This one is genuinely strange until you see the logic. **A group of people, viewed as a
group, is grammatically non-human** — and non-human nouns don't take `=enē`:

```
engar-e     shu ba-ab-ti
engar=e     shu=e ø-ba-b-ti-ø
farmer=ERG  hand=DIR VP-3N.IO-3N.A-approach-3N

'The farmers received it.'
UTAMI 4:2864 14 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.2 ex. 7
```

No plural marker on _engar_, and the verb uses the **non-human** prefix `{b}`, not the human
`{n}`. The clerk who wrote this was not thinking about individual farmers; he was thinking
about a delivery.

> "If the subject of the verb refers to an anonymous group of persons, it is nearly always
> treated as non-human and only very rarely as human."
> — Jagersma 2010: §6.2

### But _with_ coordinated nouns — yes

The one place `=enē` really is needed in the absolutive is when two nouns are joined,
because then the verb cannot tell you which of them is plural:

```
tukum-be₂ u₄-da-ta / ur-dsha-u₁₈-sha u₃ dumu-ĝu₁₀-ne ha-ba-zah₃-de₃-esh
                    [PN]  and [dumu=ĝu=enē]=ø
                              child=my=PL=ABS

'if after today Ur-Sha'usha and my children run away'
ZA 55 p.68 (ITT 5:9594) 3'–4' (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.3 ex. 43
```

Without `-ne`, you could not tell whether one child or several were involved.

And when several human nouns are coordinated, `=enē` is normally written **once, on the
last one** (Poebel 1923: §137):

```
lu₂ esh₂ gid₂ / gala-mah / agrig / lu₂ bappir₃ / ugula-ugula-ne
'the surveyors, chief lamentation-priests, stewards, brewers, and all foremen'
Uru-inimgina 4 4:2–6 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.3.3 ex. 30
```

Marking each one separately is possible but rarer: _ugula-ne / ab-ba-ne_ 'for the foremen and
elders' (VS 14:154 7:2–3).

### A useful summary

| Situation                                       | `=enē`?                   |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Non-human noun                                  | never                     |
| Human noun + numeral                            | no                        |
| Human noun in the absolutive, verb shows plural | usually no                |
| Human noun, subject of a copular clause         | no                        |
| Anonymous group / collective                    | no — treated as non-human |
| Coordinated human nouns in the absolutive       | **yes**                   |
| Human noun in the ergative, genitive, dative …  | often yes                 |

## 5. Reduplication — a plural of a different flavour

Sumerian nouns can be **reduplicated**: the whole stem is written twice.

```
kur-kur       'the foreign lands'    (Lugal-zagesi 1 1:44; Uruk, 24th c. BCE)
diĝir-diĝir   'gods'
dumu-dumu     'children'
ab-ba-ab-ba   'elders'               (TCTI 1:1030 4:2'; Lagash, 21st c. BCE)
ma-da-ma-da   'lands'
```

Unlike `=enē`, **reduplication works on both gender classes** (Jagersma 2010: §6.4). And a
few rare phonetic spellings confirm that the repetition is genuinely full — _ku-ur-ku-ur-shi_
for _kur-kur-she₃_, _ga-na-qa-na_ for _gana₂-gana₂_ 'fields',
_[t]e-er-te-re_ for _tir-tir-e_ 'forests'.

### But it is not a simple plural

Here is the crucial point, and it is easy to get wrong:

> "Every reduplicated noun expresses a plural in the sense that it refers to multiple
> entities. But the opposite is not true. A noun which refers to multiple entities does not
> have to be reduplicated and, in fact, hardly ever is."
> — Jagersma 2010: §6.4

So what does it add? Two proposals:

**Poebel (1923: 53): 'all'.** This fits a great many attestations, especially the grand
opening formulas of royal inscriptions:

```
den-lil₂ / lugal kur-kur-ra / ab-ba diĝir-diĝir-re₂-ne-ke₄
enlil [lugal [kur~kur]=ak] [abba [diĝir~diĝir]=enē=ak]=e
Enlil king lands~PL=GEN    father god~PL=PL=GEN=ERG

'Enlil, the king of all lands, the father of all gods'
En-metena 1 1:1–3 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.4 ex. 48; Zólyomi 2017: §3.1 ex. 16
```

Note that _diĝir-diĝir_ carries `=enē` **as well**. Reduplication and the plural marker are
different things and can stack.

```
dnin-hur-saĝ … ama dumu-dumu-ne
'the goddess Ninhursag, …, the mother of all children'
Gudea Statue A 1:1–3 — Jagersma 2010: §6.4 ex. 51
```

**Falkenstein (1978b: 47): distributive.** Jagersma prefers this, because 'all' does not fit
everything. On this view a reduplicated noun refers to multiple entities _while emphasising
that each is separate_ — 'here and there', 'one after another'. His clinching example comes
from an Old Sumerian donkey inventory listing the animals of two princesses:

```
anshe gurum₂-ma shid-da / dumu-dumu-ne-kam
'These are the donkeys which were counted during stock-taking and which belong to
 either of the two children (= princesses).'
Nik 1:203 4:2–3 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.4 ex. 52
```

_dumu-dumu-ne_ here cannot mean 'of all children'. There are exactly two, and the point of
the reduplication is that they are two _separate_ owners. Similarly:

```
e₂-she₃ u₄-u₄-de₃ bi₂-dib / ĝi₆-ĝi₆ ba-an-dib
'He let day after day pass for the house. He passed night after night.'
Gudea Cylinder A 8:2–3 — Jagersma 2010: §6.4 ex. 53
```

```
ib₂-ib₂-ĝu₁₀     '(each of) my hips'    (Shulgi X 21) — reduplication of just two things
```

### The one noun where reduplication _is_ a plain plural

**kur** 'mountain land, foreign land' is the exception. Singular _kur_, plural _kur-kur_ —
a clean two-way contrast. Jagersma explains why: if _kur_ is plural it _necessarily_ refers
to separate territories, so for this one word the distributive sense and the plural sense
collapse into each other (2010: §6.4).

### Reduplication was dying out

A nice detail for anyone reading across periods. In Old Sumerian Lagash texts, five human
nouns appear reduplicated: _diĝir_, _dumu_, _en_, _saĝĝa_, _ugula_. In Gudea's texts, only
_dumu_. In the Ur III texts from the same city — ten times more numerous — only four:
_ab-ba_, _dumu_, _en_, _lugal_. Frequent words like _diĝir_ and _ugula_ stop being
reduplicated altogether. "All this points to a decrease in productivity for noun
reduplication" (Jagersma 2010: §6.4).

## 6. Reduplicated adjectives

Adjectives reduplicate too, and here the plural sense is much cleaner. With **gal** 'big'
and **tur** 'small', the rule is almost mechanical: _reduplicated adjective = plural head
noun_ (Jagersma 2010: §10.3):

```
2 urudazabar₃-shu tur-tur      'two small copper hand-mirrors'   (BIN 8:388 1:1)
1 urudagab₂-la₂ tur            'one small copper hanger'         (ibid. 2:2)

51 mash gal-gal                'fifty-one full-grown he-goats'   (DP 91 1:1)
alan gal lugal-an-da           'the large statue of Lugalanda'   (DP 66 6:8)

diĝir gal-gal lagashki-a-ke₄-ne  'the great gods of Lagash'      (Cyl A 10:28)
e₂ kur gal-gen₇ …                'the temple, like a large mountain …'  (Cyl B 24:9)
```

Two by two, singular against plural. This is the most reliable plural signal in the whole
system — and note that it works perfectly well on **non-human** nouns, where `=enē` cannot go.

One warning from the Ur III scribes: they abbreviate. The vegetable names _gu₂ gal-gal_ 'big
pulses' and _gu₂ tur-tur_ 'small pulses' are very often written _gu₂ gal_ and _gu₂ tur_,
because everybody knew nobody was registering a single pea (Jagersma 2010: §10.3).

## 7. Two late arrivals: _hi-a_ and _-me-esh_

Neither is treated in our two reference grammars, but you will meet both, especially in later
texts and in Sumerograms embedded in Akkadian:

- **hi-a** 𒄭𒀀, literally 'mixed, various', used as an adjective after **non-human** nouns:
  _udu hi-a_ 'sheep (assorted)', _anshe hi-a_ '(assorted) pack-animals'. This is one of the
  few ways to force a plural reading onto a non-human noun.
- **-me-esh** 𒈨𒌍, the third person plural of the enclitic copula, tacked onto a noun:
  _lugal-me-esh_ 'kings' — originally 'they who are kings'.

There is also **didli**, written with the sign KI.MIN or DILI.DILI: a reduplicated form of
_dili_ 'single', used to mean 'individual, one by one'. Jagersma glosses it `single:PLUR`:

```
esh₃ didli urim₅ki-ke₄-ne      'individual shrines in Ur'   (UET 3:56 2–4)
mu didli ga-an-na-da-ab-sa₂    'I will match for him the individual entries!'  (TCS 1:360 13)
```

## 8. Pronouns

Personal and possessive pronouns have plural forms — but again, **only for the human class**
(Jagersma 2010: §12.4.1):

|               | singular              | plural                     |
| ------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------- |
| 1st           | **-ĝu₁₀** 'my'        | **-me** 'our'              |
| 2nd           | **-zu** 'your'        | **-zu-ne-ne** 'your (pl.)' |
| 3rd human     | **-a-ne₂** 'his, her' | **-a-ne-ne** 'their'       |
| 3rd non-human | **-be₂** 'its'        | _(same form)_              |

(Zólyomi 2017: Table 3.2)

Two of these contain `{enē}` outright: _-zu-ne-ne_ and _-a-ne-ne_, as does the independent
pronoun **a-ne-ne** 'they'.

And note the last row. The non-human `-be₂` 'its' does duty for groups of people as well,
exactly as the non-human verbal prefix `{b}` does — "when their individuality is of no
particular importance" (Jagersma 2010: §6.2).

# PART TWO — Plurality in the verb

## 9. Two different things share one name

This is the conceptual hinge of the whole page, so it is worth slowing down.

When a Sumerian verb signals "plural", it may be doing one of **two completely unrelated**
things:

**A. Nominal number, marked on the verb.** This is ordinary agreement, the same thing Latin
does in _amant_ 'they love'. The verb is counting _people_.

**B. Verbal number.** The verb is counting **events** — not participants. This category is
absent from every European language, which is why it feels alien.

> "Verbal number differs crucially from nominal number. Whereas the latter is about counting
> entities, verbal number is concerned with quantifying actions and states."
> — Jagersma 2010: §12.4.1

He quotes a lovely description of the Dravidian language Kui, which does the same thing:
special verb forms exist for "one person doing a number of things; one person doing one thing
many times; more than one person doing a number of things; more than one person doing one
thing many times" (Winfield 1928: 142–3).

Sumerian has both A and B, and they can appear in the same verb. Sections 10–12 take them in
turn.

## 10. Nominal number on the verb: three strategies

Jagersma sets out three ways to signal that a group of _persons_ is involved (2010: §13.3).
The cleanest way to see them is a set of near-identical Ur III receipts:

```
(a) SINGULAR — human prefix {n}
    ur-nigin₃-ĝar-<ke₄> / shu ba-an-ti
    ø-ba-n-ti-ø           VP-3N.IO-3SG.A-approach-3N
    'Urnigingar received this.'
    MVN 18:126 6–7 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE)

(b) PLURAL, strategy 1 — the non-human prefix {b} used of a group
    be-li₂-a-ri₂-ik / u₃ ur-nigin₃-ĝar-<ke₄> / shu ba-ab-ti
    ø-ba-b-ti-ø           VP-3N.IO-3N.A-approach-3N
    'Beli-arik and Urnigingar received this.'
    BIN 3:611 8–10 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE)

(c) PLURAL, strategy 2 — the plural person-suffix {esh}
    be-li₂-a-ri₂-ik / u₃ ur-nigin₃-ĝar-ke₄ / shu ba-an-ti-esh
    ø-ba-n-ti-esh         VP-3N.IO-3SG.A-approach-3PL
    'Beli-arik and Urnigingar received this.'
    PIOL 19:278 8–10 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE)
```

— Jagersma 2010: §13.3.1 exx. 64–65

Same formula, same city, same period, three grammatical solutions. Strategy 1 recruits the
_non-human_ prefix to stand for a plurality of persons (Waetzoldt 1992); strategy 2 adds a
plural suffix on top of a singular prefix.

**Strategy 3** is rare: an imperfective prefix `{nnē}`, written _-ne-_, marking a third
person plural human _direct object_. Jagersma finds about six certain attestations
(2010: §13.3.2, citing Wilcke 1996: 58).

### The human/non-human line is genuinely blurry

Watch what happens when the noun and the verb disagree:

```
engar-e-ne / shu ba-ab-ti
[engar=enē]=e   shu=e ø-ba-b-ti-ø
farmer=PL=ERG         VP-3N.IO-3N.A-approach-3N

'The farmers received this.'
UTAMI 4:2676 5–6 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §6.2 ex. 8
```

The noun phrase says **human plural** (`=enē`). The verb says **non-human** (`{b}`). In one
short clause the same participants are classified both ways.

> "Such discrepancies are not uncommon and show that the borderline between non-human and
> human is fluid when groups of persons are referred to."
> — Jagersma 2010: §6.2

Compare a third variant of the same formula, which goes fully human:

```
lu₂ ma₂ gal-gal-ke₄-ne / shu ba-an-ti-esh
'The men (in charge) of the large boats received this.'
MVN 7:587 obv 4–5 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE)
```

Do not expect consistency. Expect a scribe making a judgement call about whether these people
matter as individuals.

### The plural suffixes

For reference, the person suffixes that carry plural meaning (Jagersma 2010: §14):

|             | perfective / intransitive | transitive imperfective |
| ----------- | ------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 1 pl.       | **-enden**                | -enden                  |
| 2 pl.       | **-enzen**                | -enzen                  |
| 3 pl. human | **-esh**                  | **-enē**                |

That last cell is worth noting: the verbal suffix `{enē}` is the same form as the _nominal_
plural marker `{enē}`. Jagersma reconstructs both from a hypothetical obsolete demonstrative
\*/enē/ 'these, those' — a very common source for plural markers and for third-person pronouns
across languages (2010: §6.3.3, citing Diessel 1999).

## 11. Verbal number I: counting events by reduplication

Now the genuinely foreign category. Sumerian marks **plural events** by reduplicating the
verbal stem **fully**.

Zólyomi lists what "plural event" can mean: iterative, durative or intensive action; totality
of the object or the subject; distributive action — more than one participant acting
simultaneously or in succession (2017: §10.1). English usually needs an adverb: _completely_,
_all_, _each and every_, _one after another_.

```
kur         hu-mu-gam-gam
kur=ø       ha-mu-'-gam~gam-ø
lands=ABS   MOD-VEN-1SG.A-subdue~PL-3N

kalam        hu-mu-ge-en-ge-en
kalam=ø      ha-mu-'-gen~gen-ø
country=ABS  MOD-VEN-1SG.A-be.firm~PL-3N

'I subdued the foreign lands completely, I secured the land fully.'
Shulgi A 91 — Zólyomi 2017: §10.1 ex. 186; Jagersma 2010: §12.4.3 ex. 34
```

```
kug za-gin₃-be₂ ba-ta-kesh₂-kesh₂,   alan-be₂ i₃-gul-gul
'(He) collected all its precious metal and lapis lazuli from it for himself,
 and destroyed all its statues.'
Iri-kagina 5 obv 4:1–4 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §10.1 ex. 187
```

```
eg₂-ba na-ru₂-a e-me-sar-sar
'He set up inscribed stelae all over along that dike.'
En-metena 1 2:4–5 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §10.1 ex. 188
```

```
ud-ba unugki-ge giri₁₇-zal-a ud mu-da-zal-zal-le
'At that time, while Uruk passed each and every day in joy because of him …'
Lugal-zagesi 1 2:26–29 (Uruk, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §10.1 ex. 190
```

```
gu₃-de₂-a en dnin-ĝir₂-su-ra / niĝ₂ ba-na-gu-ul-gu-ul
'Gudea enlarged all things for lord Ningirsu.'
Gudea Cylinder B 2:12–13 — Jagersma 2010: §12.4.3 ex. 33
```

Both aspects can reduplicate — perfective and imperfective alike (Zólyomi 2017: §10.1).

**The phonetic evidence.** Nearly all reduplicated stems are written by simply repeating the
word sign, which hides everything. But a handful of rare phonetic spellings prove the
repetition is complete: \*hu-mu-**ge-en-ge-en\*** for /genɡen/, \*ba-na-**gu-ul-gu-ul\***,
\*mu-**ĝa₂-ar-ĝa₂-ar\***. And one Old Babylonian manuscript writes the plural stem of _dug₄_
as _du-tu₂_, reflecting a pronunciation [duttu] — which Jagersma traces back through
[tuktuk] > [tu'tu'] > [tuttu] > [duttu] from an original _dug₄-dug₄_ (2010: §12.4.3).

## 12. Verbal number II: counting participants by stem alternation

A small set of verbs — mostly verbs of position, motion, causing motion, and dying — have two
entirely different stems, one for singular and one for plural.

| Meaning         | Singular                                       | Plural                          |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| to go, come     | **ĝen** (PFV) / **du** (IPFV)                  | **er** (PFV) / **su₈-b** (IPFV) |
| to stand        | **gub**                                        | **su₈-g / shu₄-g**              |
| to sit          | **tush** (PFV) / **dur₂** (IPFV)               | **durun**                       |
| to live         | **til₃** (of persons) / **lu₅-g** (of animals) | **se₁₂** (sig₇, se₁₁)           |
| to die, to kill | **ush₂**                                       | **ug₇** (ug₅)                   |
| to bring        | **tum₂** / **de₆**                             | **lah₄ / lah₅**                 |
| to say, to do   | **dug₄**                                       | **e**                           |

(Jagersma 2010: §12.4.2; Zólyomi 2017: Table 10.1)

> **A note on 'to bring'.** This verb is a famous mess, because nearly all its forms are
> written with the same sign, DU. Zólyomi's analysis splits it by whether the object is
> countable or a mass noun; the breakthrough came from Sallaberger (2005), who found the
> form /de/ written with the TI sign in the Garshana texts (Zólyomi 2017: Lesson 10, Further
> reading). Do not expect the dictionaries to agree with one another here.

### Which participant does the counting?

**The absolutive one.** Participant number in Sumerian works on an **ergative** basis: the
stem alternates according to the number of **S** (the intransitive subject) or **P** (the
direct object) — never the transitive subject (Zólyomi 2017: §10.2).

```
S is plural — plural stem AND plural agreement:

ummaki-a, ki-ĝa₂ i₃-durunₓ(KU.KU)-ne₂-esh₂
'i-b-ø-durun-esh
'They are staying with me (lit. "at my place") in Umma.'
NWHCM 2009.174 obv 9–10 (Umma, 23rd c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §10.2 ex. 191
```

```
P is plural — plural stem, but SINGULAR agreement:

nag-suki-she₃ ha-za-num₂-e mu-na-an-lah₄
mu-nn-a-n-lah-ø
'The mayor took them (= the sheep) for him to Nagsu.'
NG 120b rev 9–10 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §10.2 ex. 192
```

Zólyomi points out the pattern: when the absolutive participant is **S**, plurality shows up
_twice_ — in the stem and in the third-person-plural suffix. When it is **P**, only the stem
shows it, since the object suffix in a perfective transitive form is `-ø`.

```
mu-na-da-shu₄-ge-esh₂
'They stepped forward to Lord Ningirsu with friendly entreaties on behalf of Gudea.'
Gudea Cylinder B 11:13–14 — Zólyomi 2017: §10.2 ex. 193
```

### The catch: these stems count _events_, not people

Here Jagersma corrects a widespread simplification. The "plural" stems are not really about
how many participants there are:

```
4 ĝurush u₄ 3-she₃ / ummaki-ta / a-pi₄-sal₄ki-she₃ / ĝiri₃-a ĝen-na
'four men for three days: who went on foot from Umma to Apisal'
UTAMI 4:2857 7–10 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §12.4.2 ex. 29
```

Four men — and the **singular** stem _ĝen_. That should be impossible if the stem counted
goers. The contrast that actually matters is this pair:

```
gu₄ ĝir₂-suki-ta ĝen-na
'(128) heads of cattle which came (as a single group) from Girsu'
ASJ 7 p. 189 3 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE)

gu₄ e₂-gal-ta er-ra
'(fourteen) oxen which went out of the palace (to several different individuals)'
BM 21486 10 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE)
```

— Jagersma 2010: §12.4.2 exx. 31–32

128 animals with the _singular_ stem; 14 with the _plural_ stem. The difference is not
headcount — it is **one going versus many goings**.

> "The plural and singular verbs primarily express verbal number, indicating the number of
> events. But as multiple events tend to imply multiple participants, in practice they mostly
> express the number of the most directly affected participant."
> — Jagersma 2010: §12.4.2

That last sentence is why the simplification survives: it usually gives the right answer.

### The two systems stack

Because stem alternation (participants) and reduplication (events) are different categories,
a plural stem can itself be reduplicated:

```
e₂-a mi-ni-shu₄-shu₄
mu-ni-n-shug~shug-ø
'He had each of them (= stone basins) stand in the temple.'
Gudea Cylinder A 23:7 — Zólyomi 2017: §10.2 ex. 194
```

_shu₄(g)_ is already the plural of _gub_ 'stand'; doubling it adds the distributive 'each of
them'.

### A caution about suppletion

You will often read that these are "suppletive" forms. Jagersma disputes the term. Suppletion
belongs to _paradigms_ — German _bin/sind_, English _go/went_ — and Sumerian verbs are not
inflected for number in the first place. _til₃_ and _se₁₂_ are better thought of as two
different lexical verbs than as two forms of one verb (2010: §12.4.2, following Corbett 2000:
258–9 and Mithun 1988: 214).

## 13. A fossil: the suffix {en}

A handful of texts, mostly third-millennium, mark the plurality of the absolutive participant
with a suffix **-/en/** attached directly to the stem:

```
a-wi-li₃ nu-banda₄ ba-ni-in-kur₉-re-en₃
ba-ni-n-kur-en-ø
'Awili, the overseer, brought them (= 8 troops) in.'
UET 3:1410 3–4 (Ur, 21st c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §10.2 ex. 196

mu-na-kurₓ(DU)-re₆-na-am₆
'These (= various animals) are (gifts) brought in for Barag-namtara.'
VS 14:179 rev 5:3 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 195
```

Almost every attestation involves the verb _kur₉_ 'enter, bring in'. Zólyomi calls `{en}`
"the vestige of an older, supplanted grammatical system" — and suggests its fingerprints are
still visible in three places (2017: §10.2):

- the plural stem **durun** 'sit', from _/dur-en/_
- the first person plural suffix **-enden**, from _/en-den/_
- the second person plural suffix **-enzen**, from _/en-zen/_

Jagersma agrees it is "the relict of an old and no longer productive suffix that used to
express a kind of plurality" (2010: §12.1, §12.5).

## 14. The reduplication trap

You have now met **three** things that are written by doubling a sign:

1. **Noun reduplication** — distributive / 'all' (§5)
2. **Full verbal reduplication** — plural events (§11)
3. **Partial verbal reduplication** — the imperfective stem (see the
   _[Perfective and imperfective](/learn/grammar_notes/perfective-imperfective.md)_ page, §5)

Numbers 2 and 3 are genuinely different operations — one repeats the whole stem, the other
only part of it — and the script spells them identically.

> "A repeated word sign can stand for two completely different types of reduplicated stems."
> — Jagersma 2010: §12.4.3

His worked example: Ur III messenger texts write _dab₅-dab₅-de₃_ 'in order to seize
(runaways)'. That looks like an imperfective stem — until you notice the many
_non_-reduplicated imperfectives of _dab₅_ elsewhere, which show the doubling here is a
plural. "Unfortunately, such cross-checks are not always possible in the case of less
frequently occurring verbs."

**How to tell them apart in practice:**

- If the verb is on Jagersma's reduplicating list (_ĝar, gi₄, ku₄, mu₂, naĝ, tuku, zig₃_ …),
  suspect **imperfective** first.
- If the clause has a plural absolutive, or an adverbial sense of 'all / each / repeatedly'
  fits, suspect **plural**.
- If both could be true, they might both be true — a plural stem can be reduplicated (§12).

## Notes on terminology

Terms marked with an asterisk\* in the text, in the order they appear.

**morphemic analysis / glossing** — Breaking a written word into its smallest meaningful
pieces (_morphemes_) and labelling each one. It is how Sumerologists show their working.

**gender class** — A grammatical sorting of nouns. Sumerian's two classes are **human**
(people and gods) and **non-human** (everything else). It has nothing to do with sex; it
governs which endings and which verbal prefixes a phrase can take — and, as this page shows,
whether it can be pluralised at all.

**clitic** — A grammatical word that leans phonologically on the word next to it but attaches
to a whole _phrase_ rather than a single word. English _'s_ is one: in _the King of Spain's
hat_, it marks the whole phrase. The Sumerian plural marker `{enē}` behaves the same way.

**plural word / number word** — A separate grammatical word that marks plurality, as opposed
to an affix glued to the noun. Dryer (1989) and Corbett (2000: 133–5) coined the terms; the
Sumerian `{enē}` is one, and it constitutes a word class with exactly one member.

**nominal number** — Counting _entities_: how many gods, how many farmers. This is what
English plural does.

**verbal number** — Counting _events_: how many separate acts of building, subduing, going.
Absent from European languages but widespread elsewhere (Dravidian, Chadic, many Native North
American languages). Sumerian marks it by reduplicating the verbal stem.

**event number / participant number** — Zólyomi's two subtypes of verbal number. _Event
number_ is plurality of the action itself (marked by full reduplication); _participant
number_ is plurality of the absolutive participant (marked by an alternate stem).

**distributive** — Referring to several things while emphasising that they are separate:
'each of', 'one after another', 'here and there'. Falkenstein's account of what reduplicated
nouns mean.

**suppletion** — A paradigm whose forms come from historically unrelated words, like English
_go / went_. Jagersma argues that the Sumerian singular/plural verb pairs are _not_ really
suppletion, because Sumerian verbs are not inflected for number to begin with.

**absolutive** — The case marking both the direct object of a transitive verb and the subject
of an intransitive one. Marked by nothing at all in Sumerian. Participant number tracks the
absolutive participant, which is what makes it an _ergative_ system.

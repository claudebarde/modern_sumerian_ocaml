# The noun cases

> **A note on spelling.** This page uses the Modern Sumerian writing conventions:
> **š → sh**, **ḫ → h**, **ŋ → ĝ**. So the terminative ending appears here as **-she₃**,
> not *-šè*; *ḫur-saŋ* as **hur-saĝ**; *šag₄* as **shag₄**. Examples quoted from the
> reference grammars have been re-spelled the same way, and their transliterations have been
> normalised to the index-number style (*é* → **e₂**, *ĝír* → **ĝir₂**).

---

## 1. A language with no prepositions

English carries most of its grammatical relations in two places: word order, and a stock of
about seventy little words we put in front of nouns.

> The king built a temple **for** the god **in** the city **with** silver **from** the palace.

Take away *for*, *in*, *with*, *from* and the sentence collapses. Sumerian has none of them.
It has **cases**: endings that go on the end of a noun phrase and do the same work.

> lugal-e diĝir**-ra** iri**-a** kug-babbar**-ta** e₂ mu-na-du₃
> king**-ERG** god**-for** city**-in** silver**-from** temple he-built-for-him

Everything English puts before the noun, Sumerian puts after it. That is the whole idea, and
once you have it, the case system stops being a list to memorise and becomes a set of
translations you already know.

Jagersma counts **twelve** cases; Zólyomi counts **eleven**, expressed by only **nine**
distinct endings. (Two endings do double or triple duty, which is why the counts differ —
see §7.) Here is Jagersma's inventory, the fuller of the two:

| Case | Human | Non-human | Rough English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genitive | ak | ak | *of* |
| Ergative | e | e | (marks the doer) |
| Absolutive | ø | ø | (marks the done-to) |
| Dative | ra | — | *to, for* |
| Directive | — | e | *at, to, on* |
| Locative | 'a | 'a | *in, on* |
| Locative 2 | — | ne | *in* (obsolete) |
| Terminative | she | she | *to, towards, for* |
| Adverbiative | esh | esh | *-ly, in the manner of* |
| Ablative | ta | ta | *from, with* |
| Comitative | da | da | *with* |
| Equative | gen | gen | *like* |

— Jagersma 2010: §7.1

Nine endings, eleven or twelve jobs, and one of the endings is silent. That is the shape of
the problem. The rest of this page works through it.

## 2. The single most important fact

**A Sumerian case marker is not a suffix on a noun. It is a clitic\* on a phrase.**

Everything else follows from this. Jagersma states it plainly:

> "The case markers are phrase-final clitics. As clitics, they are grammatical words but
> form a single phonological word with whichever word precedes. As phrase-final clitics,
> they are attached to the last word of the noun phrase to which they belong, at the very end
> of that phrase. **The word class of this last word is completely immaterial.** It can be a
> noun but also, for instance, an adjective or a verb."
> — Jagersma 2010: §7.1

If English worked this way, we would say *the king of Girsu-'s* rather than *the king's of
Girsu*. Sumerian does exactly that — and it stacks:

```
mu   ensi₂   ĝir₂-suki-ka-she₃
[mu  [ensik  [ĝirsu=ak ]=ak ]=she ]
[name[ruler  [Girsu=GEN]=GEN]=TERM]

'because of the governor of Girsu'   (lit. 'for the name of the governor of Girsu')
DTBM 99 obv 3 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.1 ex. 1
```

Three noun phrases are nested inside one another, and **all three case markers land on the
same word**, *ĝir₂-su*, because that word happens to be the last word of all three. Another,
even denser:

```
zag  shum₂    e₂-gal-ka-ta
zag  shum     [e.gal =ak =ak]=ta
border garlic [palace=GEN=GEN]=ABL

'from the border of the garlic of the palace'
VS 14:189 1:1 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §5.1 ex. 2
```

### Why this is good news

The pile-up looks intimidating, but it is doing you an enormous favour. Because a case
marker sits at the very end of its phrase, **it tells you where the phrase stops**. Jagersma
calls this the *demarcating function* (2010: §7.1). Sumerian does not use spaces, capitals
or commas. The case markers are your punctuation.

Practical reading habit: when you meet a run of signs and cannot tell where one phrase ends
and the next begins, **look for the case markers and cut there**.

### The order of the endings

More than one clitic can attach to the same word. When they do, the order never varies
(Jagersma 2010: §4.4.4):

**1. possessive pronoun → 2. plural marker {enē} → 3. case marker**

```
gudu₄-be₂-ne
[gudu.g=be =enē=e ]
[priest=this=PL =ERG]
'these priests'   (Uru-inimgina 6 1:7'; Lagash, 24th c. BCE)

dumu-ĝu₁₀-ne
[dumu =ĝu=enē=ø ]
[child=my=PL =ABS]
'my children'   (Lagash, 21st c. BCE)
```

Zólyomi describes the same thing as a five-slot **nominal template** (2017: §3.1):

| P1 | P2 | P3 | P4 | P5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **head** | **modifier** | **possessor** | **plural marker** | **case marker** |
| noun | adjective, numeral, relative clause | genitive phrase or possessive enclitic | =/enē/ | =/e/, =/ak/, =/ra/ … |

When phrases are nested, the endings of the *inner* phrase come first and the endings of the
*outer* phrase follow, en bloc:

```
ka   dumu-ne-ne-ka
[ka.g [dumu=ane=enē=ak ]='a ]
[mouth[child=her =PL =GEN]=LOC]

'in the mouth of her children'
NG 171 5 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §4.4.4 ex. 19
```

Four clitics, one written word, two different phrases. Read them from the inside out.

> **How to read the glossed examples on this page.** Line 1 is the transliteration as
> written on the tablet. Line 2 is the morphemic\* analysis — the words broken into their
> building blocks, with `=` marking a clitic boundary and `-` marking an affix boundary.
> Line 3 labels each block. `ø` is a marker that exists in the grammar but is written with
> no sign at all. Square brackets show where each noun phrase begins and ends. The
> translation is always underneath; you do not need to decode every line to follow the point.

## 3. Human and non-human

Sumerian sorts every noun phrase into two **gender classes\***: **human** and **non-human**.
This is not masculine/feminine — gods and people are human, everything else (animals,
objects, places, abstractions) is non-human.

The split matters because **several cases are restricted to one class or the other**:

- The **dative** =/ra/ is used only with human phrases (Jagersma 2010: §7.5).
- The **directive** =/e/ is used only with non-human phrases (Jagersma 2010: §7.6).
- The **locative** ='a/ is essentially non-human.
- The **ablative** =/ta/ is "as a rule … used only with non-human verbal participants"
  (Zólyomi 2017: §13.1).

So the two most common ways of saying 'to/for someone' and 'to/for something' are different
endings. Learn them as a pair:

| | human | non-human |
|---|---|---|
| indirect object 'to, for' | **-ra** (dative) | **-e** (directive / locative₃) |

And when a case simply cannot be used with a human, Sumerian reaches for a **workaround**.
The commonest is *ki* 'place' plus the genitive plus the ablative — 'from the place of X':

```
ki   ensi₂  ummaki-ta      al-la   shu ba-ti
ki   [ensi.k umma=ak]=ta   alla=e  shu=e  ø-ba-n-ti-ø
place[ruler Umma=GEN]=ABL  PN=ERG  hand=DIR VP-3N.IO-3SG.A-approach-3N

'Alla received it from the governor of Umma.'
UTAMI 3:1602 3–4 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.10
```

You cannot say *ensi₂-ta* 'from the governor'. You say 'from the governor's place'. This
idiom is everywhere in the Ur III receipts, and once you recognise *ki … -ta* as a unit it
stops looking like a sentence about a location.

## 4. The two core cases: absolutive and ergative

### Ergativity in one paragraph

English lumps together the subject of *he sleeps* and the subject of *he built it*: both are
"the subject", both are *he*. Sumerian does not. It groups the **subject of an intransitive
verb** with the **object of a transitive verb**, and treats the **subject of a transitive
verb** separately. That grouping is what *ergative* means.

Linguists label the three roles **A** (the doer of a transitive verb), **S** (the subject of
an intransitive verb), and **P** (the thing done to). The two possible groupings look like
this (Zólyomi 2017: §3.2):

| | A | S | P |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ergative** (Sumerian) | ergative | absolutive | absolutive |
| **Accusative** (English, Latin) | nominative | nominative | accusative |

### The absolutive: no ending at all

The **absolutive** is marked by nothing — a zero ending, written `ø` in glosses. It covers
the direct object of a transitive verb, the subject of an intransitive verb, the subject or
predicate of a copular clause, and the form used to address someone (Jagersma 2010: §7.4).

### The ergative: =/e/

```
ensi₂-ke₄        e₂      mu-du₃
[ensik]=e        [e]=ø   mu-n-du-ø
ruler=ERG        house=ABS  VEN-3.SG.H.A-build-3.SG.P

'The ruler has built the temple.'
Gudea Cylinder A 22:9 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §3.2 ex. 17
```

*ensi₂* 'ruler' is the doer, so it takes =/e/. *e₂* 'temple' is the thing built, so it takes
nothing at all.

```
lugal-e   ĝiri₂-ta     in-gaz
lugal=e   ĝiri=ta      'i-n-gaz-ø
king=ERG  dagger=ABL   VP-3SG.A-kill-3N

'The king killed it with a dagger.'
JCS 10 p. 28:5 6 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §4.5.1 ex. 25
```

### A wrinkle worth knowing early

Sumerian is **split-ergative\***. The case marking on nouns is ergative all the way through,
but the *verb* is not. Jagersma sets it out (2010: §11.4.3):

- **Case marking**: entirely ergative.
- **Perfective verb forms**: ergative — the prefixes mark A, a single set of suffixes marks
  both S and P.
- **Imperfective verb forms**: partly accusative, partly tripartite\* — first and second
  person A and S share one set of suffixes (accusative), while third person A and S take
  different suffixes (tripartite).

His conclusion is worth quoting, because it saves a lot of confusion later:

> "Sumerian is neither fully ergative nor fully accusative. Ignoring this can only lead to
> confusion."
> — Jagersma 2010: §11.4.3

For the noun cases, though, the rule is clean: **A takes =/e/, S and P take nothing**.

## 5. The two adnominal cases: genitive and equative

These two are different in kind from all the others. They describe a relationship between
**two nouns**, not between a noun and the verb — and this has a formal consequence Jagersma
points out (2010: §7.12): **finite verbs never contain a prefix agreeing with a genitive or
an equative phrase.** Every other case has a matching verbal prefix. These two do not,
because there is nothing in the verb for them to agree with.

### The genitive =/ak/ — and how to read -ke₄, -ka and -ra

The genitive means 'of'. Its ending is =/ak/, and it is the single most important ending to
be able to spot, because the writing almost never shows it as *ak*.

Here is the mechanism. The final /k/ of {ak} attaches to whatever ending comes next, and the
two fuse into a written syllable:

| Combination | Comes out as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| GEN + ergative/directive `=ak=e` | **-ke₄** | *lugal ki-en-gi ki-uri-**ke₄*** 'king of Sumer and Akkad' |
| GEN + locative `=ak='a` | **-ka** | *kisal dnin-ĝir₂-su lugal-ĝa₂-**ka*** 'in the courtyard of Ningirsu, my lord' |
| GEN + dative `=ak=ra` | **-ra** | *dnin-ĝir₂-su, e₂-ninnu-**ra*** 'for Ningirsu in the E-ninnu' |
| GEN + terminative `=ak=she` | **-ka-she₃** | *mu ensi₂ ĝir₂-su-**ka-she₃*** 'because of the governor of Girsu' |
| POSS + GEN + absolutive `=ane=ak=ø` | **-na** | *e₂ lugal-**na*** 'his master's temple' |

(Sources: Ur-Namma 14 6; Gudea Statue B 8:24; En-ana-tum I 19 1–2; DTBM 99; Gudea Cylinder A
24:8 — all Zólyomi 2017: §3.3, exx. 19–24.)

**So when you see -ke₄ at the end of a chain, do not look for a verb "ke".** You are looking
at a genitive followed by an ergative. This single insight unlocks a very large fraction of
royal inscriptions, which are almost entirely strings of the form *X of Y of Z did such and
such*.

Jagersma adds an important warning: when the /k/ of {ak} would end a closed syllable, the
script simply does not write it, and you have to infer it from what follows (2017: §3.3
makes the same point). In *e₂-ninnu-ra*, the dative appears in its full form *ra* rather than
the reduced *r* — and the only reason the preceding syllable can be closed is that an
invisible genitive /k/ is sitting there.

Genitives nest freely:

```
gud   diĝir-re₂-ne-ke₄
[gud  [diĝir=enē=ak ]=e ]
[ox   [god  =PL =GEN]=ERG]
'the oxen of the gods'
Iri-kagina 1 4:9 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §4 ex. 28

sipad  udu   siki-ka-ke₄-ne
[sipad [udu  [siki=ak ]=ak ]=enē=e ]
[shepherd[sheep[wool=GEN]=GEN]=PL =ERG]
'the shepherds of the wool-bearing sheep'
Iri-kagina 1 3:18 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §3.1 ex. 14
```

### The anticipatory (left-dislocated) genitive

Normally a noun phrase is unbroken. There is exactly one exception, and it involves the
genitive: the possessor can be **pulled out to the front** of the clause, leaving a
possessive pronoun behind to hold its place (Jagersma 2010: §5.1, §7.2.4; Zólyomi calls it
the *left-dislocated genitive*, 2017: §4.2).

```
e₂-a         den-ki-ke₄   ĝesh-hur-be₂     si   mu-na-sa₂
[e.j=ak]     [enki.k]=e   [ĝeshhur=be]=e   si=ø  ø-mu-nna-n-sa-ø
house=GEN    Enki=ERG     plan=its=DIR     ?=ABS VP-VEN-3SG.IO-3SG.A-be.equal-3N

'Enki straightened out for him the ground-plan of the temple.'
(lit. 'Of the house … its plan')
Gudea Cylinder A 17:17 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §5.1 ex. 3
```

English does this too, in speech: *That house — its roof is leaking.* If a stray genitive
seems to belong to nothing, look further down the line for a *-be₂* or *-a-ne₂*.

### The equative =/gen/

The equative means 'like' or 'as … as'. It is written with the sign GIM, transliterated
**gen₇** (older publications write *gim* or *gin₇*). It attaches to the **standard** — the
thing being compared *to* (Zólyomi 2017: §3.4).

```
ni₂-be₂     kur-kur-ra    tug₂-gen₇   im-dul₄
ni=be=ø     [kur~kur]='a  [tug]=gen   'i-m-dul-ø
awe=its=ABS lands~PL=LOC  cloth=EQU   VP-VEN-cover-3N

'Its awesomeness covered all the lands like a cloth.'
Gudea Cylinder A 29:18 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.12 ex. 329
```

```
urim₂ki-e   gud-gen₇    saĝ an-she₃   mu-da-ab-il₂
urim=e      [gud]=gen   saĝ=ø an=she  mu-n-da-b-il-ø
Ur=ERG      bull=EQU    head=ABS sky=TERM  VEN-3.SG.H-COM-3.SG.NH.A-raise-3.SG.P

'Ur raised its head to the sky like a bull because of him.'
Lugal-zagesi 1 2:30–32 (Uruk, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §3.4 ex. 27
```

Two details that trip people up:

**The compared thing does not copy the case.** In the Gudea example above, the thing being
covered is in the locative, but *tug₂* 'cloth' is only equative — not equative *plus*
locative. Jagersma notes that as a result "it is sometimes necessary to supply a preposition
in translating a Sumerian comparison" (2010: §7.12).

**Sumerian does not distinguish 'as … as' from 'like'.** English has two constructions —
*You are as kind as Bau* (sameness of extent) versus *Ninurta roars like a storm* (sameness
of manner). Sumerian uses =/gen/ for both, and only the meaning tells them apart
(Zólyomi 2017: §3.4). The personal name **dba-u₂-gen₇-a-ba-sag₉** 'Who-is-as-kind-as-Bau?'
(Lagash, 24th c. BCE) is the first type; the two examples above are the second.

## 6. The adverbial cases

These are the cases that do the work of English prepositions. Each one also has a matching
prefix inside the verb — see §9.

### Dative =/ra/ — 'to, for' (people only)

```
dnin-ĝir₂-su-ra
[ninĝirsuk]=ra
DN=DAT.H
'for the god Ningirsu'
Gudea Statue B 7:24 — Zólyomi 2017: §3.1 ex. 11

dnin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄   iri-ka-ge-na-ra   ki-gub       mu-na-pad₃
ninĝirsuk=e        irikagenak=ra     kigub=ø      ø-mu-nna-n-pad-ø
Ningirsu=ERG       PN=DAT            position=ABS VP-VEN-3SG.IO-3SG.A-find-3N

'Ningirsu found a position for Irikagena.'
Uru-inimgina (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.4 ex. 109
```

After an open syllable, =/ra/ is reduced to /r/, and a syllable-final /r/ was not written
before the very end of the third millennium (Zólyomi 2017: §3.3). So *dnin-shubur,
nin-a-ne₂* 'for Ninshubur, his lady' (Gudea 72 1–2) **is** a dative even though nothing
visible marks it.

### Directive =/e/ — 'at, on, to' (things only)

Homophonous with the ergative, and cognate with it: Jagersma argues the two go back to a
single form, the transitive construction having grown out of an older passive in which the
directive marked the agent phrase — the way English *by* means both nearness (*the table by
the window*) and agency (*arrested by the police*) (2010: §7.3).

```
lu₂ ummaki-ke₄     e   ki-sur-ra-ke₄        izi     ba-shum₂
[lu umma=ak]=e     [e.g kisura=ak]=e        izi=ø   ø-ba-n-shum-ø
man Umma=GEN=ERG   dike border=GEN=DIR      fire=ABS VP-3N.IO-3SG.A-give-3N

'The Ummaite set fire to the border dike.'
Uru-inimgina 16 1:1–5 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.6.1 ex. 136
```

Two further uses worth knowing:

**Purpose clauses.** The standard way to say 'in order to' is an imperfective participle in
the directive:

```
e₂ kug     ru₂-de₃           gu₂-be₂     mu-shi-ib₂-il₂
[e kug]=ø  [ru-ed]=e         gu=be=ø     ø-mu-n-shi-b-il-ø
house holy=ABS erect-IPFV=DIR neck=its=ABS VP-VEN-3SG-to-3N.A-lift-3N

'It raised its neck towards him in order to build the holy temple.'
Gudea Cylinder A 1:16 — Jagersma 2010: §7.6.2 ex. 154
```

**Topic marking**, translated 'as for':

```
idim-e        a-na       nu-zu
idim=e        ana=ø      nu='i-n-zu-ø
spring=DIR    what=ABS   NEG=VP-3SG.A-know-3N

'As for the spring, what does he not know?'
VS 10:189 8 (21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.6.2 ex. 159
```

### Locative ='a/ — 'in, on'

Jagersma treats this as one case with a wide range; Zólyomi splits it into **locative₁**
('in, inside') and **locative₂** ('on, upon'), on the strength of the *different verbal
prefixes* they take (2017: §14). Both agree about what the endings look like on the noun.

```
shag₄ iri-na-ka         ha-ne₂-gaz-ze
[shag [iri=ane=ak]]='a  ha-ni-n-gaz-e
heart city=his=GEN=L1   MOD-L1-3.SG.H.P-kill-3.SG.A

'May (the people) kill him in the middle of his city!'
En-metena 1 6:28–29 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: §14.2 ex. 378
```

```
a-hush-a       shu   be₂-bar
[ahush]='a     shu=ø ø-bi-n-bar-ø
Ahush=LOC      hand=ABS VP-3N:on-3SG.A-open-3N

'He plundered the Ahush.'   (lit. 'opened the hand on the Ahush')
Uru-inimgina 16 2:2–3 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.7.2 ex. 181
```

The locative stretches much further than the verbal prefixes do. Jagersma lists (§7.7.2):

- **the material something is made from**: *kug-sig₁₇ kug-babbar-ra shu mu-na-ne-tag* 'He
  decorated it with gold and silver for him' (Enmetena 16 2:3–4)
- **the place of an event, circumstantially**: *lagashki-a ĝesh e-tag* 'This has been
  sacrificed in Lagash' (DP 53 2:11–12)
- **time**, which the verbal prefixes never do: *u₄ ezem-ma-ka* 'on the day of the
  festival'; *iti du₆-kug-ga* 'in the month Duku'
- **the content of a speech act**: *e₂-a-ne₂ ru₂-ba mu-na-du₁₁* 'He spoke to him about his
  temple and the building of it' (Gudea Cylinder A 1:19)

Because the range is so wide, a single clause can carry several locatives at once:
*ezem munu₄ gu₇ dnanshe-ka niĝin₆ki-na e₂-gal-la ba-ku₄* 'During Nanshe's Malt-Eating
Festival, it was brought into the palace in Nigin' (DP 164; Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — three of
them, one temporal and two spatial.

### Terminative =/she/ — 'to, towards, for'

The semantic opposite of the ablative. It covers destination in space, destination in time,
and — metaphorically — purpose.

```
e₂-muhaldim-she₃        'to the kitchen'      (Nik 1:162 1:4; Lagash, 24th c.)
lagashki-she₃           'to Lagash'           (Gudea Statue D 4:13)
u₄ ul-la-she₃           'forever'  (lit. 'until the day of the bud')  (Uru-inimgina 12 14')
4 ĝuruš u₄ 24-she₃      'four labourers for 24 days'   (UTAMI 3:1605 1; Umma, 21st c.)
a-ra₂ 3-kam-ma-ash      'for the third time'  (PDT 1:502 8; Drehem, 21st c.)
```

With verbs of making, it means 'into, so as to become':

```
ĝesh-eren-be₂        ig gal-she₃        mu-dim₂
[eren=be]=ø          [ig gal]=she       ø-mu-n-dim-ø
cedar=this=ABS       door big=TERM      VP-VEN-3SG.A-create-3N

'He fashioned these cedars into a big door.'
Gudea Statue B 5:45–47 — Jagersma 2010: §7.8.2 ex. 226
```

And it expresses purpose or function, 'for' or 'as':

```
dba-u₂ …-ke₄   iri-ka-ge-na      nam-sipa-she₃         mu-du₂
bau …=ak=e     irikagenak=ø      namsipad=she          ø-mu-n-dud-ø
Bau …=GEN=ERG  PN=ABS            shepherdship=TERM     VP-VEN-3SG.A-give.birth-3SG

'Bau gave birth to Irikagena in order that he be shepherd.'
Uru-inimgina (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.8.2 ex. 228
```

### Ablative =/ta/ — 'from, out of, with'

Its basic sense is source or origin:

```
u₄ ummaki-ta        i₃-im-ĝen-na-a
u₄.d umma=ta        'i-m-ĝen-ø-'a='a
day Umma=ABL        VP-VEN-go-3SG-NOM=LOC
'when he came from Umma'
SACT 1:154 6 (Drehem, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.10 ex. 263
```

Paired with the terminative, it gives a span — in space or in time:

```
e-be₂    id₂-nun-ta      gu₂ eden-na-she₃            eb-ta-ni-e₃
e.g=be=ø idnun=ta        [gu eden=ak]=she            'i-b-ta-ni-n-e-ø
dike=its=ABS Idnun=ABL   bank plain=GEN=TERM         VP-3N-from-in-3SG.A-go.out-3N

'He extended its dike from the Idnun to the Guedena.'
En-metena 28 2:1–3 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.10 ex. 276

iti nesaĝ₂-ta / iti ddumu-zi-she₃
'from the month Nesag to the month Dumuzi'   (UTAMI 3:1692 4–5; Umma, 21st c.)
```

Its second big job is **instrument**, 'with, by means of' — which is why older grammars call
it the *ablative-instrumental*:

```
lugal-e   ĝiri₂-ta      in-gaz
'The king killed it with a dagger.'   (JCS 10 p. 28:5 6; Drehem, 21st c. BCE)

inim ensi₂-ka-ta
'by order of the governor'   (lit. 'by the word of the governor')   (TENS 104 8)

a₂ dnanshe-ta / a₂ dnin-ĝir₂-su-ka-ta
'by the power of Nanshe and by the power of Ningirsu'   (Gudea Statue D 4:2–3)
```

And a third, 'in addition to':

```
nam-ensi₂ lagashki-ta / nam-lugal kishki / mu-na-ta-shum₂
'In addition to the rulership over Lagash, she gave him the kingship over Kish.'
E-ana-tum 5 6:2–5 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.10 ex. 285
```

### Comitative =/da/ — 'with, together with'

Unlike most adverbial cases, this one is used freely with both human and non-human phrases
(Jagersma 2010: §7.11.2).

```
muhaldim-da   ĝen-na
[muhaldim]=da ĝen-ø-'a
cook=COM      come-NFIN-NOM
'who came together with the cook'   (MVN 7:36 rev 1; Lagash, 21st c. BCE)

ummaki-da     dam-ha-ra    e-da-ak
umma=da       damhara=ø    'i-b-da-n-'ak-ø
Umma=COM      battle=ABS   VP-3N-with-3SG.A-make-3N
'He did battle with Umma.'   (En-metena 28 1:25–27; Lagash, 25th c. BCE)
```

It also shifted from accompaniment to **reason**, 'because of':

```
e₂-an-na-tum₂ … dnin-ĝir₂-su-ka-da   dnin-ĝir₂-su   mu-da-hul₂
'Ningirsu rejoiced over Eannatum, the … of Ningirsu.'
E-ana-tum 1 5:1–5 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §7.11.2 ex. 320
```

And it turns up in a curious time expression, 'X years ago', literally 'since its Xth with
the years': *mu-da 15-ta* 'fifteen years ago' (NG 34 6–7; Lagash, 21st c. BCE).

### Adverbiative =/esh/ — 'in the manner of'

This one makes adverbs. Jagersma treats it as a twelfth case; Zólyomi rejects that and calls
it "a derivative morpheme, which turns an expression into an adverb of manner"
(2017: §3.1, §5.2). Either way you will meet it.

```
e₂ lugal-na         zid-de₃-esh₂     mu-du₃
[e lugal=ane=ak]=ø  [zid]=esh        ø-mu-n-du-ø
house king=his=GEN=ABS right=ADV     VEN-3.SG.H.A-build-3.SG.P

'He built his master's temple in the appropriate manner.'
Gudea Cylinder A 24:8 — Zólyomi 2017: §5.2 ex. 68
```

**The practical problem**: after a consonant the adverbiative is /esh/ and the terminative is
/she/, but after a vowel both are written with the same sign ŠE₃ (*-she₃* or *-esh₂*).
Jagersma is blunt about the consequence: "some instances of the adverbiative case may have
been misread as terminatives" (2010: §7.9). When a *-she₃* makes no sense as 'to' or 'for',
try 'in the manner of'.

### Locative 2 =/ne/ — the fossil

Jagersma lists a twelfth case, **"Locative 2"**, with the marker =/ne/. It is
"all but obsolete. It only occurs in a few fixed expressions and in the so-called
'pronominal conjugation'" (2010: §7.1). He argues it is cognate with the verbal prefix
{ni} 'in', and that the ordinary locative ='a/ took over its jobs at some point before our
texts (2010: §7.7.2). Zólyomi labels the same fossil **L4, "the archaic locative enclitic
=/ne/"** (2017: abbreviations).

**Do not confuse Jagersma's "Locative 2" with Zólyomi's "locative₂".** They are entirely
different things. See the next section.

## 7. Where the grammars disagree

This is the part of Sumerian case grammar that causes the most needless frustration. The
languages *of the grammars* differ more than the analyses do. Here is a map:

| The thing itself | Jagersma 2010 | Zólyomi 2017 | Thomsen / Foxvog |
|---|---|---|---|
| =/e/ on a non-human indirect or oblique object | **directive** | **locative₃** | **locative-terminative** |
| ='a/ meaning 'in, inside' | **locative** | **locative₁** | locative |
| ='a/ meaning 'on, upon' | **locative** (with the prefix {e} 'on') | **locative₂** | locative |
| =/ra/ on a human where a thing would take ='a/ or =/e/ | dative, or an **oblique object** | **locative₂ / locative₃** | dative |
| =/ta/ | **ablative** | **ablative** | **ablative-instrumental** |
| =/esh/ | **adverbiative** (a case) | not a case — an adverb-forming morpheme | — |
| archaic =/ne/ | **"Locative 2"** | **L4, archaic locative** | — |
| Total cases counted | **12** | **11** | 10 (Thomsen) |

Three things to take from this:

1. **Jagersma's one locative = Zólyomi's two.** Zólyomi splits them because they take
   different verbal prefixes (/ni/ versus /i, e/), even though the noun ending is the same.
   Jagersma keeps them together because the noun ending is the same, and handles the
   difference in his chapter on the verbal prefixes instead. Both are describing the same
   language.
2. **"Directive" and "locative₃" are the same case.** So is Thomsen's and Foxvog's
   "locative-terminative", which is the name you will meet most often in older literature and
   in sign lists.
3. **The numbering is a trap.** Jagersma's "Locative 2" is an obsolete fossil written
   =/ne/. Zólyomi's "locative₂" is a live, common case written ='a/ or =/ra/. Same words,
   opposite referents.

There is also a genuine substantive disagreement. Jagersma holds that the dative is
**human-only** (2010: §7.1, §7.5); Zólyomi gives it a non-human form =/e/ as well
(2017: Table 3.3). Where Zólyomi sees a non-human dative, Jagersma sees a directive. On this
site we follow Jagersma, but if you read Zólyomi you will see the other label.

## 8. What the writing hides

Sumerian orthography is not a phonetic transcription. Several case markers are routinely
invisible, and knowing *which* ones saves enormous confusion. Jagersma lays out the causes
(2010: §7.4):

**The script does not write vowel length.** The ergative and directive =/e/ contract with a
preceding vowel. So after a word ending in a vowel, both cases can be completely unmarked.
"Old Sumerian orthography never expresses the directive case marker after a vowel"
(2010: §7.6.1). From the Gudea period onward, scribes sometimes indicate the resulting long
vowel with a **plene spelling**\* — repeating the vowel sign:

```
mu-be₂-e   an-zag-ta   kur-kur-re   gu₂ im-ma-si-si
mu=be=e    …           kur~kur=e    gu=ø  …
name=its=DIR           lands~PL=ERG neck=ABS

'From the borders of heaven the foreign lands will gather for its fame.'
Gudea Cylinder A 9:18 — Jagersma 2010: §7.6.1 ex. 137
```

That extra *-e* in *mu-be₂-e* is the directive making itself visible.

**The script does not usually write syllable-final consonants**, especially early. After a
vowel the dative reduces to /r/ and the comitative to /d/, and both then vanish from the
writing until the very end of the third millennium.

**The genitive /k/ disappears** whenever it would close a syllable.

The practical upshot: **a noun phrase that looks unmarked is not necessarily absolutive.**
Before concluding that something is a direct object, check whether an ergative, directive,
dative, comitative or genitive could be hiding there — and let the verb decide. Which brings
us to the other half of the system.

---

## 9. The other half: the verb agrees

Sumerian marks its cases **twice** — once on the noun phrase, once inside the verb. Zólyomi
puts it directly: "In Sumerian cases are distinguished … not solely by nominal case-markers;
the verbal affixes also play an essential role in the identification of cases" (2017: §3.1).

Each adverbial case has a reserved slot in the verbal prefix chain:

| Case | Noun ending | Verbal prefix | Slot (Zólyomi) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dative | =ra (human) | /a/ | S7 |
| Comitative | =da | /da/ | S8 |
| Ablative | =ta | /ta/ | S9 |
| Terminative | =she | /shi/ | S9 |
| Locative₁ | ='a | /ni/ | S10 |
| Locative₂ | ='a, =ra | /i/ or /e/ | S10 |
| Locative₃ (directive) | =e, =ra | /i/ or /e/ | S10 |

Two consequences fall straight out of this table.

**Slots compete.** The ablative and terminative share S9; the three locatives share S10. A
verb cannot host two of them at once. When a clause needs both, Sumerian re-routes one
participant into the dative instead — Zólyomi's **dative promotion** (2017: §15.4).

**Agreement is not equally strict.** With the dative it is absolute: "a noun phrase in the
dative always occurs together with a corresponding dative prefix in the verbal prefix-chain"
(Zólyomi 2017: §12.1). With the ablative it is loose: ablative phrases "may occur regularly
without any corresponding prefix", and conversely an ablative prefix may appear with no noun
phrase at all, merely modifying the verb's meaning (Zólyomi 2017: §13.1).

And, as noted in §5, the **genitive and equative have no verbal counterpart at all** — they
relate nouns to nouns, so there is nothing in the verb for them to agree with.

For reading, this redundancy is a gift. When the noun ending is invisible (§8), the verbal
prefix is often still there, and it tells you what case to reconstruct.

## Notes on terminology

Terms marked with an asterisk\* in the text, in the order they appear.

**clitic** — A grammatical word that cannot stand alone and leans phonologically on the word
next to it, but that attaches to a whole *phrase* rather than to a single word. English
*'s* is a clitic: in *the King of Spain's hat*, it marks the whole phrase *the King of
Spain*, not the word *Spain*. All Sumerian case markers are clitics of exactly this kind.

**morphemic analysis / glossing** — Breaking a written word into its smallest meaningful
pieces (*morphemes*) and labelling each one. It is how Sumerologists show their working.

**gender class** — A grammatical sorting of nouns. Sumerian's two classes are **human**
(people and gods) and **non-human** (everything else). It has nothing to do with sex; it
governs which case endings and which verbal prefixes a phrase can take.

**split-ergative** — Said of a language that follows an ergative pattern in one part of its
grammar and a different pattern elsewhere. Sumerian's noun cases are ergative throughout,
but its imperfective verb forms are not.

**tripartite** — A pattern in which all three of A (transitive subject), S (intransitive
subject) and P (object) are marked differently, rather than any two being grouped together.

**plene spelling** — Writing a vowel sign twice to show that the vowel is long — for example
*mu-be₂-e* rather than *mu-be₂*. It is one of the few ways Sumerian orthography can reveal a
contracted case marker.

**adnominal / adverbial case** — An *adnominal* case relates one noun to another (genitive,
equative). An *adverbial* case relates a noun to the verb, the way an English prepositional
phrase does (dative, locative, terminative, ablative, comitative, directive).

**standard (of comparison)** — In *as kind as Bau*, "Bau" is the standard: the thing the
comparison is measured against. Sumerian puts the standard in the equative case.

**dative promotion** — Sumerian verbs have a single slot for a locative prefix. When a clause
would need two at once, one participant is re-cast as an indirect object and marked with the
dative instead. A verb's usual case frame can therefore be temporarily overridden.

**oblique object** — In Jagersma's terminology, a participant marked inside the verb by a
special set of *oblique-object prefixes*. It takes the dative ending when human and the
directive ending when non-human — the same endings as an indirect object — and only the
verbal prefix distinguishes the two.

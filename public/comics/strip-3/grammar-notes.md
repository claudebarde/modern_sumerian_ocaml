# Strip 3 — "Duga"

A very short strip: one question, one answer, five signs between them. But the
question word hides a piece of grammar that the answer then puts on display — and
along the way we run into something Sumerian simply does not have.

> **How to read the boxes**
>
> Each balloon gets a card with the cuneiform, the transliteration and the
> translation. Underneath, every meaningful piece of the sentence is explained in
> its own box.
>
> Modern Sumerian conventions: **sh** for *š*, **h** for *ḫ*, **ĝ** for *ŋ*.

---

## Panel 1

> ## 𒌨𒂠𒈬 𒈨𒀀
> ### Urgirĝu mea?
> **Transliteration** ur-gir₁₅-ĝu₁₀ me-a
> **Translation** "Where is my dog?"

| Sign | 𒌨 | 𒂠 | 𒈬 | 𒈨 | 𒀀 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name | UR | EŠ₂ | MU | ME | A |
| Reads | ur | gir₁₅ | ĝu₁₀ | me | a |

### Breaking it apart

```
ur.gir=ĝu  =Ø    me   =ʾa
dog   =my  =ABS  what =LOC
"Where is my dog?"
```

> **`ur-gir₁₅-ĝu₁₀` — "my dog"**
>
> Straight from strip 1, unchanged: the compound `ur-gir₁₅` "dog", with the 1sg
> possessive `-ĝu₁₀` attached to the end of the whole phrase.

> **`me-a` — "where?"**
>
> This is not an unanalysable little word meaning "where". Take it apart and you
> find two pieces: an interrogative stem `me`, and **`-a`, the locative case
> marker**. Literally, it means **"at what place"**.
>
> Which means the question already contains the ending that the answer is going
> to need. Hold that thought until panel 3.

> **What isn't there: a verb, and a copula**
>
> "Where is my dog?" has no word for "is". That is not an omission — it is the
> rule.
>
> Sumerian interrogative clauses with a nominal predicate **do not take the
> copula**. You have seen that copula in `Nindaĝum` and `Kasham`, and you might
> expect it here. It is systematically absent from questions of this shape.
> Jagersma's example has exactly our structure with a different noun:
> `mar-za-ĝu₁₀ me-a`, "Where are my functions?"
>
> Noun, possessive, question word. Nothing else. That is a complete Sumerian
> sentence.

> **A note for later**
>
> The rule has a second half, which the next strip will show you: when an
> interrogative stands at the **front** of the sentence instead of the end, it
> *does* take the copula — `a-na-am₃`, "what is it?" Position decides. For now,
> notice only that `me-a` is sitting at the end and carries nothing.

## Panel 3

> ## 𒂁𒀀
> ### Duga!
> **Transliteration** dug-a
> **Translation** "In the jar!"

| Sign | 𒂁 | 𒀀 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | DUG | A |
| Reads | dug | a |

### Breaking it apart

```
dug =ʾa
jar =LOC
"In the jar."
```

> **`dug` — "jar"**
>
> A clay pot or jar — one of the commonest objects in the Sumerian world, and one
> of the commonest words in the corpus. It has been standing in the corner of
> Ka-kug's courtyard since the very first strip without ever being named. Now it
> has a name, and a dog in it.

> **`-a` — the locative, "in"**
>
> Here is the payoff. The `-a` on `dug-a` is the **same ending** you already met
> welded into `me-a`.
>
> | | | |
> |---|---|---|
> | `me` | `-a` | at what place → **where?** |
> | `dug` | `-a` | at the jar → **in the jar** |
>
> The question and the answer rhyme, because they are in the same case. Sumerian
> marks the role a noun plays in a sentence by adding an ending to it, and `-a`
> is the ending for "in, at, on". This is your first Sumerian case — and you met
> it, without knowing, in panel 1.

> **Why the answer is one word**
>
> `Duga!` is an answer fragment, exactly as "in the jar!" is in English. Nobody
> repeats the whole sentence when replying to a question.
>
> That is also the safe way to say it. A full sentence — "my dog is in the jar" —
> would properly want the verb `ĝal`, "to be somewhere", which the series does
> not introduce until strip 5. The one-word answer sidesteps it, and lands better
> anyway.

> **For the curious: Sumerian has no word for "here"**
>
> You might expect the answer to a "where?" question to be *here* or *there*.
> Sumerian cannot oblige.
>
> It has almost no adverbs of place at all. **"Here" and "hither" are expressed
> by a prefix on the verb** — the *ventive* `mu-`, meaning "toward the speaker".
> There is no free-standing word to put in a speech balloon.
>
> "There" does exist, but it is not a word either — it is a small noun phrase:
> `ki-ba`, built from `ki` "place", a demonstrative, and the same locative `-a`
> we have just learned. Literally, "in this place".
>
> So when a Sumerian tells you where something is, they either name the place, as
> Ka-kug does, or they build the direction into the verb. We will come back for
> that ventive prefix later — it is how you say "come here".

## What this strip teaches

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `-a` | the **locative** case: "in, at, on" — your first case ending |
| `me-a` | "where?" — literally `me` "what" + the locative `-a` |
| `dug` | (clay) jar |

| Idea | |
|---|---|
| Questions of this shape take **no copula** | `ur-gir₁₅-ĝu₁₀ me-a`, not *`me-a-àm` |
| Case endings attach to the noun | `dug` → `dug-a` |
| There is **no word for "here"** | Sumerian builds it into the verb instead |

## Try it yourself

1. "Where is my bread?" (`ninda` = bread)
2. "In the house." (`e₂` = house)
3. Why does the question in panel 1 have no word for "is"?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. `ninda-ĝu₁₀ me-a` — **Nindaĝu mea?**
2. `e₂-a` — **Ea.** The same locative ending, on a different noun. (`e₂-a` is
   well attested.)
3. Because Sumerian interrogative clauses with a nominal predicate do not contain
   a copula. The `-am₃` you learned in strip 2 belongs to statements, not to
   questions of this shape.

</details>

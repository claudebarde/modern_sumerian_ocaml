# Strip 4 — "Abam / Anam"

Kakug asks two questions in this strip and gets two bad answers. The questions
use different words — and the reason they differ is one of the most
characteristic things about the Sumerian language.

> **How to read the boxes**
>
> Each balloon gets a card with the cuneiform, the transliteration and the
> translation. Underneath, every meaningful piece of the sentence is explained in
> its own box.
>
> Modern Sumerian conventions: **sh** for *š*, **h** for *ḫ*, **ĝ** for *ŋ*.

---

## Panel 1

> ## 𒀀𒁀𒀀𒀭
> ### Abam?
> **Transliteration** a-ba-am₃
> **Translation** "Who is it?"

| Sign | 𒀀 | 𒁀 | 𒀀𒀭 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name | A | BA | A·AN |
| Reads | a | ba | am₃ |

### Breaking it apart

```
a.ba =Ø   =am
who  =ABS =be:3SG.S
```

> **`a-ba` — "who?"**
>
> This is the question you ask about a **person**. Sumerian has a second question
> word, `a-na`, for everything else — and the choice between them is not a matter
> of style. The language sorts every noun into one of two classes and gives each
> class its own interrogative.
>
> Kakug cannot see what is moving at the edge of the courtyard, so he assumes a
> visitor and asks accordingly. He is about to be corrected.

> **`-am₃` — "it is"**
>
> The copula, for the third time in the series. You met it in strip 1 hidden
> inside `Nindaĝum`, and plainly in strip 2 as `Kasham`. Here it attaches to a
> question word: literally "who is it?"
>
> All three balloons in this strip end in `-am₃`. Watch the ending stay still
> while the word in front of it changes.

> **Why the question word comes first**
>
> In the previous strip, the question word sat at the **end** — `Urgirĝu mea?`,
> "my dog where?" — and there was no copula. Here the question word comes
> **first**, and it takes one.
>
> That is a real rule rather than an accident. An interrogative normally stands
> immediately before the verb in Sumerian, not at the start of the sentence as in
> English. There is a second construction that moves it to the front, and that
> construction attaches the third-person copula to it. So `me-a` at the end takes
> nothing; `a-ba` at the front takes `-am₃`.

## Panel 2

> ## 𒀀𒈾𒀀𒀭
> ### Anam?
> **Transliteration** a-na-am₃
> **Translation** "What is it?"

| Sign | 𒀀 | 𒈾 | 𒀀𒀭 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name | A | NA | A·AN |
| Reads | a | na | am₃ |

### Breaking it apart

```
a.na =Ø   =am
what =ABS =be:3SG.S
```

> **`a-na` — "what?"**
>
> The same question, asked of a different kind of thing. `a-ba` was for a person;
> `a-na` is for everything that is not a person.
>
> Notice how little changes. The two words share a first sign, share the copula,
> and differ by one syllable in the middle. In Sumerian, "who" and "what" are
> near-twins.

> **The big idea: Sumerian sorts the world in two**
>
> Sumerian nouns have gender — but not masculine and feminine. The division is
> **human** against **non-human**.
>
> The human class is small and specific. It contains personal and divine names,
> kinship terms like `šeš` "brother" and `dumu` "child", occupations like
> `dub-sar` "scribe", and the generic words `diĝir` "god", `lu₂` "man",
> `munus` "woman". That is essentially all.
>
> **Everything else is non-human** — plants, objects, rivers, towns, abstract
> ideas, and, importantly for this strip, **animals**. Sheep, donkeys, dogs and
> frogs are all grammatically *things*.
>
> So when the shadow at the edge of panel 1 turns out to be the dog, Kakug's
> first question was aimed at the wrong class, and his second question has to
> switch. He asks **who**, gets a dog, and then has to ask **what**.

> **This will follow you everywhere**
>
> The human / non-human split is not a curiosity confined to question words. It
> runs through the whole grammar:
>
> - only human nouns take the plural marker — for non-human nouns, singular and
>   plural look the same;
> - the dative case is used only with human nouns, the directive only with
>   non-human ones;
> - the verb marks human and non-human participants with different prefixes.
>
> This strip is your first sight of a system you will meet in every Sumerian
> sentence you ever read.

> **For the curious: the two words are inside out**
>
> Everywhere else in Sumerian, the pronoun elements that refer to people contain
> an **/n/**, and the ones that refer to things contain a **/b/**.
>
> In these two interrogatives it is exactly reversed: human `a-**b**a`,
> non-human `a-**n**a`.
>
> Jagersma reads this as evidence that the /n/ ~ /b/ gender contrast is a
> relatively late development in the language, and that these two little words
> preserve an older arrangement whose original logic can no longer be recovered.
> They are a fossil sitting in plain sight.

## Panel 3

> ## 𒉈𒍝𒍝𒀀𒀭
> ### Bizazam
> **Transliteration** bi-za-za-am₃
> **Translation** "It's a frog."

| Sign | 𒉈 | 𒍝 | 𒍝 | 𒀀𒀭 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name | NE | ZA | ZA | A·AN |
| Reads | bi₂ | za | za | am₃ |

### Breaking it apart

```
bizaza =Ø   =am
frog   =ABS =be:3SG.S
```

> **`bi-za-za` — "frog"**
>
> A real Sumerian word, and a pleasing one to say. It also turns up as the name
> of a kind of stone and a kind of fish, which is a common pattern — Sumerian
> liked to name things after what they resembled.
>
> The first sign, 𒉈, is the sign NE, read here as **bi₂**. One sign, several
> readings: you met this already with 𒁉, which reads `kaš` in "beer" and `bi`
> elsewhere. The subscript number tells you which reading is meant.

> **`-am₃` — "it is", contracting again**
>
> `bizaza` ends in a vowel, so the vowel of the copula runs together with it into
> a single long vowel — exactly what happened in strip 2 with `a-am₃`, "it is
> water". The cuneiform marks the length by writing the extra A sign.
>
> We letter it **Bizazam** rather than *Bizazaam*. The rule we follow: double the
> vowel only when the word would otherwise disappear into the ending. `A` needed
> the help. `Bizaza` does not.

> **The answer matches the question**
>
> Read the three balloons in order and listen to the endings:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | `a-ba-am₃` | who is it? |
> | `a-na-am₃` | what is it? |
> | `bi-za-za-am₃` | it is a frog |
>
> Sumerian answers a copular question with a copular clause. The reply repeats
> the shape of the question with the unknown filled in — the question word drops
> out and the answer takes its place, with the same ending still attached.

## What this strip teaches

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `a-ba` | "who?" — the interrogative for **human** nouns |
| `a-na` | "what?" — the interrogative for **non-human** nouns |
| `-am₃` | "it is" — the same copula as strips 1 and 2, now on three different words |

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `bi-za-za` | frog |

And one idea worth more than any of the words: **Sumerian divides all nouns into
human and non-human**, and that division shapes the pronouns, the cases, the
plural and the verb.

## Try it yourself

1. You hear someone knocking at the door of your house. Do you ask **Abam?** or
   **Anam?**
2. Your dog drops a clay jar at your feet. Which do you ask?
3. How would you say "It is bread"? (`ninda` = bread)

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Abam?** — a person is knocking, so the human interrogative.
2. **Anam?** — a jar is a thing, so the non-human interrogative. And note that
   even the dog itself would be an `a-na`, not an `a-ba`.
3. `ninda-am₃` — **Nindam**. `ninda` ends in a consonant, so nothing contracts
   and the copula stays in its plain form, just as in `Kasham`.

</details>

## A note on sources

Every form in the first two balloons is attested. `a-ba-am₃` and `a-na-am₃` both
appear in the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary's lists of attested
spellings (`aba[who?]`, 621 attestations; `ana[what?]`, 891).

The third is a regular construction rather than a quoted spelling. `bi-za-za`
"frog" is well attested — in the absolutive, in the genitive, with the equative
`-gin₇` — but **not with the enclitic copula attached**. The form
`bi-za-za-am₃` is built by the ordinary rules and is identical in shape to the
attested `a-am₃` of strip 2, but no tablet we have happens to carry it. We think
it is worth saying so plainly rather than letting a reconstructed form pass as a
quotation.

The grammar in these notes follows:

- **A. H. Jagersma**, *A Descriptive Grammar of Sumerian* (2010) — §6.2 on the
  human and non-human gender classes and their consequences for number, case and
  the verb; §8.5 on the interrogative pronouns, their position in the clause, and
  the reversed /n/ ~ /b/ pattern; §30.3 on interrogative clauses without a copula.
- **G. Zólyomi**, *An Introduction to the Grammar of Sumerian* (2017) — Lesson 5
  on the interrogative pronouns, including the rule that a sentence-initial
  interrogative takes the third person singular enclitic copula.

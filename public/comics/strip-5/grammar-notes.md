# Strip 5 — "Bizaza min iĝal"

The same sentence three times, with one word changing. That is the joke, and it
is also the lesson — because in Sumerian, almost nothing else in the sentence
*can* change.

This strip also quietly brings in the first **verb** of the series.

> **How to read the boxes**
>
> Each balloon gets a card with the cuneiform, the transliteration and the
> translation. Underneath, every meaningful piece of the sentence is explained in
> its own box.
>
> Modern Sumerian conventions: **sh** for *š*, **h** for *ḫ*, **ĝ** for *ŋ*.

---

## Panel 1

> ## 𒉈𒍝𒍝 𒁹 𒉌𒅅
> ### Bizaza dish iĝal.
> **Transliteration** bi-za-za diš i₃-ĝal₂
> **Translation** "There is one frog."

| Sign | 𒉈 | 𒍝 | 𒍝 | 𒁹 | 𒉌 | 𒅅 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name | NE | ZA | ZA | DIŠ | NI | IG |
| Reads | bi₂ | za | za | diš | i₃ | ĝal₂ |

### Breaking it apart

```
bizaza diš =Ø    ʾi -ĝal     -Ø
frog   one =ABS  VP -be.there-3SG.S
```

> **`bi-za-za` — "frog"**
>
> Our friend from strip 4, who arrived once and has evidently told everyone.

> **`diš` — "one"**
>
> Written 𒁹, a single wedge — and that is all there is to it.
>
> You might expect Sumerian to leave "one" out, the way English says "there's a
> frog" rather than "there is one frog". It can: a bare `bizaza` would be
> perfectly good. But Ka-kug is counting, and a count starts at one. This is the
> same instinct that makes an English speaker say "one, two, three" rather than
> "a, two, three".
>
> Keep an eye on this wedge. You will meet it again, slightly changed, in panel 3.

> **`i₃-ĝal₂` — "there is"**
>
> This is the first **verb** in the series, and it is the gentlest one the
> language has to offer.
>
> It comes in two pieces. The stem is `ĝal`, "to be there, to be at hand, to be
> available, to exist". In front of it sits `i₃-`, a **vocalic prefix**: it marks
> the word as a finite verb and has no meaning you can translate. Behind the stem
> there is nothing at all, because the third person subject is marked with silence.
>
> Two pieces, one fixed shape. Learn `iĝal` as a unit and you can say that
> something exists.

> **`ĝal` is not the copula**
>
> English uses "to be" for two quite different jobs, and Sumerian keeps them
> apart:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Bizazam** | "It **is** a frog" — saying what something is |
> | **Bizaza iĝal** | "There **is** a frog" — saying that something exists |
>
> The first is the enclitic copula `-am₃` you have had since strip 2. The second
> is a real verb with a stem and a prefix. If you remember only one thing from
> this page, make it this: `-am₃` identifies, `ĝal` locates.

> **What isn't there: a word for "there"**
>
> English needs a placeholder — *there* is a frog, *it* is raining. Sumerian
> needs nothing. `bizaza i₃-ĝal₂` is two words: "frog" and "exists". The whole
> weight of the English construction sits inside one verb.

## Panel 2

> ## 𒉈𒍝𒍝 𒈫 𒉌𒅅
> ### Bizaza min iĝal.
> **Transliteration** bi-za-za min i₃-ĝal₂
> **Translation** "There are two frogs."

| Sign | 𒉈𒍝𒍝 | 𒈫 | 𒉌𒅅 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reads | bi-za-za | min | i₃-ĝal₂ |
| Means | frog | two | there is |

### Breaking it apart

```
bizaza min =Ø    ʾi -ĝal     -Ø
frog   two =ABS  VP -be.there-3SG.S
```

> **`min` — "two"**
>
> Written 𒈫, which is two wedges. Sumerian numerals are among the easiest things
> in the writing system to read, because for the small numbers you can simply
> count the marks.

> **The numeral comes after the noun**
>
> `bizaza min` — literally "frogs two". This is the opposite of English, and it
> is not optional: an attributive numeral **always** follows its noun.
>
> You may see the reverse in photographs of tablets, where administrative lists
> write "2 sheep, 1 goat" with the number first. That is a scribal convention for
> visual clarity in accounts, the way we write "3 × milk" on a shopping list. In
> the spoken language the noun came first, and wherever the grammar shows through
> — in the way case endings attach — the numeral is following its noun.

> **The noun does not change**
>
> One frog was `bizaza`. Two frogs are also `bizaza`. There is no plural form,
> and there never will be. Only the numeral in front of the verb changed.
>
> This follows directly from strip 4. Sumerian sorts nouns into **human** and
> **non-human**, and only the human class marks plurality. A frog is non-human,
> so it has exactly one form however many of them there are. To say how many, you
> count — which is precisely what this strip is doing.

## Panel 3

> ## 𒉈𒍝𒍝 𒐕 𒉌𒅅
> ### Bizaza ĝesh iĝal.
> **Transliteration** bi-za-za ĝeš₂ i₃-ĝal₂
> **Translation** "There are sixty frogs."

| Sign | 𒉈𒍝𒍝 | 𒐕 | 𒉌𒅅 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reads | bi-za-za | ĝeš₂ | i₃-ĝal₂ |
| Means | frog | sixty | there is |

### Breaking it apart

```
bizaza ĝeš =Ø    ʾi -ĝal     -Ø
frog   60  =ABS  VP -be.there-3SG.S
```

> **`ĝeš₂` — "sixty", and why it is funny**
>
> Ka-kug has stopped counting. Sixty is the round number of the Sumerian world,
> the way "a hundred" is of ours — *there are a hundred frogs in here* doesn't
> mean anyone counted a hundred.
>
> The reason sixty plays that role is that the entire Sumerian number system runs
> on it, as the table further down this page shows.

> **The wedge from panel 1, grown**
>
> Look at the sign for sixty: 𒐕. It is the same single wedge as `diš` "one" in
> panel 1, only larger.
>
> That is not an error, and it is the neatest thing on this page. In a base-60
> system, sixty is where the count starts over — so it is written with the same
> mark as one, raised a level. The size is what tells them apart, exactly as the
> position of a digit tells 1 from 10 in our own notation.
>
> Sixty frogs, then, are written with almost the same sign as one frog. Ka-kug's
> afternoon has scaled up by a full order of the Sumerian number system.

> **The verb does not change either**
>
> `i₃-ĝal₂` is identical in all three panels. Sumerian's finite verb has separate
> plural forms, but they refer only to the **human** class — so for frogs, one
> and sixty look exactly alike.
>
> Read the three balloons together and notice how little moves:
>
> | | | |
> |---|---|---|
> | bizaza | **dish** | iĝal |
> | bizaza | **min** | iĝal |
> | bizaza | **ĝesh** | iĝal |
>
> The noun is fixed, the verb is fixed, and the number carries everything. That is
> not a simplification for the comic. That is how the language works.

## What this strip teaches

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `i₃-ĝal₂` | "there is, there are" — the existential verb, your first finite verb |
| `diš` | one |
| `min` | two |
| `ĝeš₂` | sixty |

| Idea | |
|---|---|
| Numerals **follow** their noun | `bizaza min`, "frogs two" |
| Non-human nouns have **no plural** | one frog and sixty frogs are both `bizaza` |
| `-am₃` identifies, `ĝal` locates | "it is a frog" vs "there is a frog" |

## The numerals in full

### One to ten

| | Sumerian | Sign | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **diš** (also **aš**) | 𒁹 | |
| 2 | **min** | 𒈫 | |
| 3 | **eš** | 𒐈 | |
| 4 | **limmu** | 𒐉 | |
| 5 | **ya** | 𒐊 | |
| 6 | **aš** | 𒐋 | ← 5 + 1 |
| 7 | **imin** | 𒅓 | ← 5 + 2 (*ya* + *min*) |
| 8 | **ussu** | 𒐍 | ← 5 + 3 (*ya* + *eš*) |
| 9 | **ilimmu** | 𒑆 | ← 5 + 4 (*ya* + *limmu*) |
| 10 | **u** | 𒌋 | |

Look at six through nine. You can still see the hand inside them: *imin* is
five-and-two, *ussu* five-and-three, *ilimmu* five-and-four. Somewhere behind the
Sumerian number words is a much older habit of counting on fingers.

### The tens

| | Sumerian | Sign | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | **niš** | 𒎙 | |
| 30 | **ušu** | 𒌍 | ← 3 × 10 |
| 40 | **nimin** | 𒐏 | ← 2 × 20 |
| 50 | **ninnu** | 𒐐 | ← 2 × 20 + 10 |

Forty is "two twenties" and fifty is "two twenties and a ten" — a vigesimal
pocket sitting inside a sexagesimal system, much like French *quatre-vingts*.

### Sixty and beyond

| | Sumerian | Sign | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | **ĝeš₂** | 𒐕 | the base |
| 600 | **ĝešu** | 𒐞 | ← 60 × 10 |
| 3,600 | **šar** | 𒊹 | ← 60 × 60 |
| 36,000 | **šaru** | 𒐬 | ← 3,600 × 10 |
| 216,000 | **šargal** | 𒐲 | ← 60 × 60 × 60, "big šar" |

The system alternates ×6 and ×10 as it climbs, with sixty as the pivot. Sixty is
a convenient base because so many numbers divide it cleanly — 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10,
12, 15, 20 and 30 all go in without remainder, which matters enormously when you
are dividing rations, fields and silver without fractions.

And it never went away. Sixty seconds in a minute, sixty minutes in an hour, 360
degrees in a circle: those divisions descend directly from Mesopotamian
arithmetic. Every time you read a clock you are using a Sumerian number.

### Fractions

| | Sumerian | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| ⅓ | **šušana** | 𒑚 |
| ⅔ | **šanabi** | 𒑛 |
| ⅚ | **kingusila** | 𒑜 |

### Ordinals

Sumerian builds "first, second, third" from the cardinal plus an ending written
**-kam**: `min-kam` "second", `eš-kam` "third", `limmu-kam` "fourth", `ya-kam`
"fifth", `imin-kam` "seventh".

That ending is not arbitrary. It is the genitive "of" followed by the copula "it
is" — so *the third* is, literally, **"it is of three"**. Sumerian ordinals are
little copular clauses, which means you already knew half of this from strip 2.

## Try it yourself

1. "There are three frogs."
2. "There is bread." (`ninda` = bread)
3. "There are ten dogs." (`ur-gir₁₅` = dog)

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. `bi-za-za eš i₃-ĝal₂` — **Bizaza esh iĝal.**
2. `ninda i₃-ĝal₂` — **Ninda iĝal.**
3. `ur-gir₁₅ u i₃-ĝal₂` — **Urgir u iĝal.** Note that `ur-gir₁₅` does not change
   for ten any more than `bizaza` did for sixty — dogs are non-human too.

</details>

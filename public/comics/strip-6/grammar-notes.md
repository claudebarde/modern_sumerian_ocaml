# Strip 6 — "Nindane mea?"

One word, three owners. This strip has no new vocabulary at all — every balloon is
the word for "bread" you have had since strip 1, with a different ending on it
each time.

It also introduces someone new, and she is here for a grammatical reason.

> **How to read the boxes**
>
> Each balloon gets a card with the cuneiform, the transliteration and the
> translation. Underneath, every meaningful piece of the sentence is explained in
> its own box.
>
> Modern Sumerian conventions: **sh** for *š*, **h** for *ḫ*, **ĝ** for *ŋ*.

---

## The new face

**Ninkugzu** — `nin-ku₃-zu` 𒎏𒆬𒍪 — is Ka-kug's neighbour. Like most Sumerian
names, hers is a small sentence: `nin` "lady" + `kug` "pure" + `zu` "know", so
roughly **"the lady is wise"**. It is an attested name, found 120 times in the
corpus.

She and Ka-kug share a word. His name is `ka-kug`, "pure mouth"; hers contains the
same `kug`, "pure, shining, silver". Sumerian names were built from ordinary
vocabulary, and the same pieces turn up again and again.

She does not speak in this strip. She is here because of what she makes possible:
**she is a person.** Everything below depends on that.

## Panel 1

> ## 𒃻𒈬
> ### Nindaĝu.
> **Transliteration** ninda-ĝu₁₀
> **Translation** "My bread."

| Sign | 𒃻 | 𒈬 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | GAR | MU |
| Reads | ninda | ĝu₁₀ |

```
ninda =ĝu =Ø
bread =my =ABS
```

> **`-ĝu₁₀` — "my"**
>
> Familiar from strip 1, where it gave us `ur-gir₁₅-ĝu₁₀` "my dog" and
> `ninda-ĝu₁₀-um` "that's my bread". Possession in Sumerian is an ending glued to
> the end of the noun phrase, not a separate word: *bread-my*.
>
> Keep an eye on the **first sign**, 𒃻. It is the same in all three balloons.
> Only what follows it changes.

---

## Panel 2

> ## 𒃻𒍪
> ### Nindazu.
> **Transliteration** ninda-zu
> **Translation** "Your bread."

| Sign | 𒃻 | 𒍪 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | GAR | ZU |
| Reads | ninda | zu |

```
ninda =zu   =Ø
bread =your =ABS
```

> **`-zu` — "your"**
>
> The second person singular, and it works exactly like `-ĝu₁₀`: one ending, same
> position, no separate pronoun. `ninda-zu` "your bread", `gu₄-zu` "your ox".

> **He is talking to the dog — and that is allowed**
>
> You may have expected a problem here. Strip 4 taught that Sumerian sorts nouns
> into **human** and **non-human**, and that animals are firmly on the non-human
> side: the dog is a *what*, not a *who*.
>
> But that split only operates in the **third** person. There is no gender in "I"
> or "you". Whoever you are addressing — a person, a dog, a jar, a god — the
> ending is `-zu`.
>
> Which is worth stating the other way round, because it is the useful half:
> Sumerian cares about the human / non-human distinction when you talk **about**
> someone, never when you talk **to** them.

## Panel 3

> ## 𒃻𒉌 𒈨𒀀
> ### Nindane mea?
> **Transliteration** ninda-ni me-a
> **Translation** "Where is her bread?"

| Sign | 𒃻 | 𒉌 | 𒈨 | 𒀀 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name | GAR | NI | ME | A |
| Reads | ninda | ne | me | a |

```
ninda =ane =Ø    me   =ʾa
bread =her =ABS  what =LOC
```

> **`-ane` — "his" and "her", both at once**
>
> The third person singular possessive — and here is the thing worth carrying
> away: **Sumerian has no separate word for "his" and "her".**
>
> `ninda-ane` is "his bread" or "her bread" indifferently. The language records
> that the owner is a **person**; it does not record which sex that person is. We
> translate it "her" in this strip only because the loaf happens to belong to
> Ninkugzu.
>
> A note on the spelling: the cuneiform sign 𒉌 can be read *ni* or *né*, so you
> will see this ending transliterated both ways. We follow Jagersma, who
> reconstructs the morpheme as `{ane}` and writes it `-né` — hence **Nindane**.
> ePSD2 prints the same sign as `ni`, which is why its attested form appears as
> `ninda-ni`. Same sign on the tablet, two modern habits.

> **Had the bread belonged to the dog**
>
> It would have been **`ninda-bi`**, not `ninda-ane`. The third person is where
> the human / non-human split bites: `-ane` for people, `-bi` for everything else.
>
> So this one ending is doing two jobs at once. It tells you *whose* the bread is,
> and it tells you that the owner is a human being. There is no way to say "her
> bread" in Sumerian without also announcing that she is a person.
>
> And that is why Ninkugzu had to walk into the courtyard. The dog cannot trigger
> `-ane`. Neither can the frog. For five strips this ending was simply out of
> reach.

> **`me-a` — "where?", returning**
>
> Straight from strip 3, and behaving the same way: the noun phrase, then `me-a`,
> and **no copula**. Sumerian questions of this shape don't take one.
>
> Remember that `me-a` is itself two pieces — `me` "what" plus the locative `-a`,
> literally "at what place". So panel 3 is quietly revising the first case ending
> you ever met.

## The whole series

Three panels have given you three of these. Here is the full set:

| | ending | |
|---|---|---|
| 1st singular | `-ĝu₁₀` | my |
| 2nd singular | `-zu` | your |
| 3rd singular, **human** | `-ane` | his, her |
| 3rd singular, **non-human** | `-bi` | its |
| 1st plural | `-me` | our |
| 3rd plural, human | `-anene` | their |

All of them attach the same way — to the end of the noun phrase, after everything
else. And three of them are attested on this very word: ePSD2 lists `ninda-ĝu₁₀`,
`ninda-zu`, `ninda-ni` and `ninda-bi` among the recorded spellings of `ninda`.

## The joke, for the record

The dog was given a loaf. It is still on the table in panel 3, untouched. What the
dog ate was the loaf belonging to somebody else.

Which means the punchline of this strip is grammatical: **the dog ate the wrong
suffix.**

---

## What this strip teaches

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `-ĝu₁₀` | my — 1st person singular |
| `-zu` | your — 2nd person singular, no gender |
| `-ane` | his / her — 3rd person singular, human only |
| `-bi` | its — 3rd person singular, non-human |

| Idea | |
|---|---|
| No "his" vs "her" | Sumerian marks the owner as human, not as male or female |
| Gender lives in the 3rd person only | You address a dog as `-zu` exactly as you would a person |
| Possessives are phrase-final endings | `ninda-zu`, never a separate word |

## Try it yourself

1. "His dog." (`ur-gir₁₅` = dog)
2. "Its bread" — the bread belonging to the frog.
3. "Where is my beer?" (`kaš` = beer)

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. `ur-gir₁₅-ane` — **Urgirane.** The owner is a person, so `-ane`, regardless of
   whether he or she is meant.
2. `ninda-bi` — **Nindabi.** A frog is non-human, so `-bi`.
3. `kaš-ĝu₁₀ me-a` — **Kashĝu mea?** Noun phrase, possessive, question word, and
   no copula — exactly the shape of panel 3.

</details>

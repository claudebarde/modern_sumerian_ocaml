## 1. The problem Sumerian had to solve

English makes new verbs all day long. We turn nouns into verbs by simply using them
(_to butter_, _to google_), we bolt on affixes (_central_ → _to centralize_), we glue verbs
together (_to typewrite_). The set of English verbs has no upper limit.

Sumerian could do none of this.

> "In Sumerian, the number of verbs is limited. There are only a few hundred verbal stems
> and Sumerian lacks the grammatical means to create new ones. There is not a single
> word-formation rule for creating a new verbal stem on the basis of another word."
> — Jagersma 2010: §12.2

A few hundred verbs is not many for a civilisation that ran temples, courts, canals and
international trade. So Sumerian did something else: it paired an existing verb with a fixed
noun, and let the _pair_ carry a new meaning.

| Noun                | +   | Verb                          | =   | Compound verb                   |
| ------------------- | --- | ----------------------------- | --- | ------------------------------- |
| **igi** 'eye'       |     | **bar** 'to be aside, direct' |     | **igi—bar** 'to look at'        |
| **ki** 'place'      |     | **aĝ₂** 'to measure out'      |     | **ki—aĝ₂** 'to love'            |
| **a₂** 'arm, power' |     | **aĝ₂** 'to measure out'      |     | **a₂—aĝ₂** 'to order, instruct' |
| **shu** 'hand'      |     | **bala** 'to cross'           |     | **shu—bala** 'to alter, change' |
| **shu** 'hand'      |     | **tag** 'to touch'            |     | **shu—tag** 'to decorate'       |
| **ĝesh** 'wood'     |     | **tag** 'to touch'            |     | **ĝesh—tag** 'to sacrifice'     |

Look at what the same verb does in different company. **aĝ₂** 'measure out' gives 'love'
with _ki_ and 'command' with _a₂_. **tag** 'touch' gives 'decorate' with _shu_ and
'sacrifice' with _ĝesh_. These are Jagersma's own illustrations (2010: §12.2), and they make
the essential point: **you cannot reliably work out the meaning from the parts.** A compound
verb has to be learned as a unit, the way you learn English _to look up_, _to look after_,
_to look into_ — three unrelated meanings from one verb and three small words.

The dash we write between the two parts (_igi—bar_) is a modern convention. It is a signal
to the reader: _these two words belong together, look them up as a pair._

### What makes it a compound verb and not just a noun plus a verb

The technical criterion is that the noun is **non-referential\***. In _igi—bar_ 'to look
at', there is no actual eye anywhere in the sentence:

> "A compound verb is the combination of a verb and a noun phrase where the noun phrase is
> non-referential and consequently forms a semantic unit with the verb. […] Here the word
> _igi_ 'eye' does not refer to an existing entity; rather, it modifies the meaning of the
> verb."
> — Zólyomi 2017: §15.2

Compare two Sumerian phrases:

- _ninda gu₇_ 'to eat bread' — here _ninda_ 'bread' refers to real bread. Ordinary verb,
  ordinary object.
- _igi bar_ 'to look at' — here _igi_ 'eye' refers to nothing. Compound verb.

You can count bread, own bread, describe bread as good or stale. You cannot do any of that
to the _igi_ in _igi—bar_. That is the test.

### A word about the name

**"Compound verb"** is the traditional name in Sumerology and the one used by the electronic
Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary (ePSD2), which tags the two halves _compound verb nominal
element_ and _compound verb verbal element_.

Jagersma objects to it. In linguistics a _compound_ is a single word made of two words
(_greenhouse_); the Sumerian construction is two separate words that happen to mean one
thing. He therefore uses **"phrasal verb"** instead, borrowing the term from Jeremy Black:

> "The Sumerian 'compound verbs' only have a unitary meaning, but their composite parts (a
> verb and a noun) remain separate words. They are therefore no compounds in the linguistic
> sense."
> — Jagersma 2010: §12.2

Zólyomi keeps the traditional name but warns that "the definition of compound verbs is
controversial in Sumerology" (2017: Lesson 15). Attinger, Karahashi and others have all
proposed slightly different boundaries for the category.

**We use "compound verb" on this site**, because that is what you will meet in dictionaries
and text editions. But keep Jagersma's point in mind, because it has a consequence you will
feel immediately when you start reading tablets.

## 2. The consequence: the two parts can drift apart

Because the noun and the verb are two separate words, **other material can come between
them**, and the noun can pick up modifiers of its own. Jagersma demonstrates this with a
pair of passages from the Gudea corpus (2010: §5, exx. 7–8).

First, the plain version:

```
den-lil₂-e      en dnin-ĝir₂-su-she₃     igi zid       mu-shi-bar
enlil=e         en ninĝirsuk=she         igi zid=ø     ø-mu-n-shi-n-bar-ø
Enlil=ERG*      lord Ningirsu=TERM*      eye true=ABS* VP-VENT-3SG-to-3SG.A-be.out-3N.S/DO

'Enlil looked at lord Ningirsu with favour.'   (lit. 'directed a true eye towards')
Gudea Cylinder A 1:3 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE)
```

Now the same compound verb, stretched across three lines:

```
u₄ dinana-ke₄ / igi nam-ti-ka-ne₂ / mu-shi-bar-ra-a
u₄.d inana.k=e   igi nam.ti.l=ak=ane=ø   ø-mu-n-shi-n-bar-ø-'a='a

'when Inanna had looked at him with her life-giving eyes'
Gudea Statue C 2:11–13 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE)
```

Here _igi_ carries a genitive phrase (_nam-ti-la-k_ 'of life') **and** a possessive suffix
(_-ani_ 'her'), and sits a full line away from _bar_.

> **How to read the glossed examples on this page.** Line 1 is the transliteration as
> written on the tablet. Line 2 is the morphemic\* analysis — the same words broken into
> their building blocks, with `=` before an ending and `-` before a prefix or suffix inside
> the verb. Line 3 (where given) labels each block. `ø` marks an ending that is present in
> the grammar but written with no sign at all. You do not need to decode every line to
> follow the argument; the translation is always underneath.

**Practical advice for reading.** When you meet a verb form you cannot place, scan backwards
through the clause for a bare noun — especially a body part — that might be its partner.
Three habits will save you a lot of time:

- The nominal element normally stands **immediately before** the finite verb, so that is
  where you look first.
- It is normally **bare**: no plural marker, no numeral, no demonstrative. If a noun before
  the verb looks suspiciously undressed, suspect a compound verb.
- Its usual grammatical role is that of **direct object** (the absolutive\* case). This
  matters, and §4 explains why.

## 3. Two verbs that do most of the work: _dug₄_ and _ak_

Two Sumerian verbs turn up so often as the verbal half of a compound that they have
effectively stopped meaning anything on their own. Their job is simply to convert a noun
into a predicate. Linguists call this a **light verb\***; Zólyomi uses the term of them
directly (2017: §15.2), and Jagersma notes that compounds with these two "are extremely
numerous" (2010: §12.2).

If you know these two patterns, you can decode hundreds of forms.

### ak 'to do, make'

Take almost any noun, add _ak_, and you have a verb.

```
ummaki-da       dam-ha-ra       e-da-ak
umma=da         damhara=ø       'i-b-da-n-'ak-ø
Umma=COM*       battle=ABS      VP-3N-with-3SG.A-make-3N.S/DO

'(He) did battle with Umma.'
Enmetena 28 1:25–27 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §12.2 ex. 2
```

```
a-sha₃-be₂          ur-diĝir-ra-ke₄      kiĝ₂           bi₂-in-na
a.sha.g=be=e        urdiĝira.k=e         kiĝ=ø          ø-bi-n-'ak-ø
field=its=DIR*      PN=ERG               work=ABS       VP-3N-3SG.A-make-3N.S/DO

'Urdingir worked their field.'   (lit. 'made work on their field')
TCS 1:148 13–14 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §12.2 ex. 3
```

More of the same kind: **ĝesh-ga-rig₂—ak** 'to comb, card' (from _ĝesh-ga-rig₂_ 'comb'),
**en-nu-uĝ₃—ak** 'to keep watch' (from _en-nu-uĝ₃_ 'watch'), **nam-lugal—ak** 'to reign as
king' (from _nam-lugal_ 'kingship'). Notice that these are barely idiomatic at all — 'to do
comb' really does mean 'to comb'. That is the difference between a light verb and a genuine
idiom like _ki—aĝ₂_ 'to love'.

### dug₄ 'to do, to say'

```
a-sha₃-ge       a          du₁₁-ga
a.sha.g=e       a=ø        dug-ø-'a
field=DIR       water=ABS  do-NFIN*-NOM*

'who irrigated the field'   (lit. 'who did water to the field')
TENS 83 1–2 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §12.2 ex. 5
```

```
ki-sur-ra dnin-ĝir₂-su-ka-ta / a-ab-she₃ /
mashkim              lu₂       nu-e
mashkim=ø            lu₂=e     nu='i-'e-e
commissioner=ABS     man=ERG   NEG=VP-do:IPFV-3SG.A:IPFV

'From Ningirsu's border to the sea, nobody acts as a commissioner anymore.'
Uru-inimgina 4 9:22–25 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §12.2 ex. 4
```

Keep that second example in mind. The verb there is written **e**, not _dug₄_ — because _e_
is the imperfective form of _dug₄_. We come to that in §5.

One honest caveat: nobody yet knows why Sumerian has _two_ light verbs, or how a scribe
chose between them. Jagersma states the position plainly: "it is as yet unclear in what way
the two verbs differ from each other" (2010: §12.2). You simply learn which one goes with
which noun.

## 4. Where does the real object go?

Here is the puzzle that trips up beginners.

In _igi—bar_ 'to look at', the direct-object slot has already been used up by _igi_ 'eye'.
So what case does the **thing you are looking at** take? Zólyomi calls this participant the
**semantic object**:

> "The meaning of the compound verbs often requires another verbal participant which is
> affected by the verbal action. This participant functions as a kind of 'semantic object'
> in addition to the grammatical object. The case of this semantic object varies; **it
> appears to depend idiosyncratically on the verb**."
> — Zólyomi 2017: §15.2

_Idiosyncratically_ is the important word. **There is no rule.** You learn the case together
with the verb, exactly as a learner of English has to learn _depend_ **on**, _listen_
**to**, _approve_ **of** — patterns nobody can predict from meaning alone.

A good dictionary entry for a compound verb therefore has three parts: the pair, the
meaning, **and the case frame**. Here are the frames that are actually attested.

### Locative₃ / directive (`-e`) — the commonest

```
en arattaki-ke₄       gig-e         igi bi₂-in-du₈
en aratta=ak=e        gig=e         igi=ø  b-i-n-du-ø
lord GN=GEN=ERG       wheat=L3      eye=ABS  3.SG-L3-3.SG.A-open-3.SG.P

'The lord of Aratta looked at the wheat.'
Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta 554 — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 453
```

### Locative₂ (`-'a`)

```
ki-sur-ra lagashki-ka,   lu₂ urim₅ki-ka-ke₄,    inim bi₂-ĝar
kisura lagash=ak='a      lu urim=ak=e           inim=ø  b-i-n-ĝar-ø
territory GN=GEN=L2      man GN=GEN=ERG         word=ABS  3.SG-L2-3.SG.A-place-3.SG.P

'The man from Urim raised a claim on the territory of Lagash.'
Utu-heĝal 1 6–8 (Uruk, 22nd c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 454
```

### Dative (`-ra`) — for persons

```
iri-ka-gi-na-ra      ki mu-na-aĝ₂
irikaginak=ra        ki=ø  mu-nn-a-n-aĝ-ø
PN=DAT*              place=ABS  VEN-3.SG.H-DAT-3.SG.A-measure-3.SG.P

'The goddess Bau … loves Iri-kagina.'
Iri-kagina 14p 1–2 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 455
```

Jagersma gives the same frame from a century earlier: _lugal ki an-na-aĝ₂-ĝa₂-ne₂_ 'his
master who loves him' (Enannatum I 33 4:1; Lagash, 25th c. BCE — 2010: §17.3 ex. 71).

### Terminative (`-she₃`)

This is the standard frame for **igi—bar**:

```
den-lil₂-e   en dnin-ĝir₂-su₂-she₃   igi zid mu-shi-bar
'Enlil looked at lord Ningirsu with approval.'
Gudea Cylinder A 1:3 — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 456
```

With **shu—ti** 'to receive' the terminative does something different — it marks the person
**from whom** you receive:

```
lugal-a-ne₂ sizkur₂ ra₂-zu-ne₂ gu₃-de₂-a-ash₂,
en dnin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄ shu ba-shi-ti

'His master, Lord Ningirsu, accepted his prayer and supplication from Gudea.'
Gudea Cylinder A 2:21–22 — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 365
```

This is the single most useful frame to memorise, because _shu—ti_ is the commonest compound
verb in the whole language: it is the verb of every receipt in the Ur III archives.

### Comitative (`-da`)

```
den-lil₂-da     a-ba      a₂ mu-da-an-aĝ₂
enlil=da        aba=e     a=ø  mu-n-da-n-aĝ-ø
DN=COM          who=ERG   arm=ABS  VEN-3.SG.H-COM-3.SG.A-measure-3.SG.P

'Whoever instructed divine Enlil?'
Shulgi G 8 — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 457
```

### Indirect object

A few compound verbs treat the second participant as an indirect object\*. **gu₃—de₂** 'to
call for' is one:

```
e₂-e           lugal-be₂       gu₃ ba-de₂
e.j=e          lugal=be=e      gu₃=ø  ø-ba-de-e
house=DIR      owner=its=ERG   voice=ABS  VP-3N.IO-pour-3SG.A:IPFV

'For a house its owner called.'
Gudea Cylinder A 1:10 — Jagersma 2010: §17.3 ex. 69
```

### Why you sometimes see a dative where you expect a locative

One complication is worth knowing before it confuses you. A Sumerian verb form has only one
slot for a locative prefix. If a clause needs two of them at once, something has to give — and
what gives is that one participant gets **promoted to the dative** instead. Zólyomi calls this
**dative promotion\*** (2017: §15.4).

**nam—tar** 'to decree a fate' normally puts the person whose fate is decreed in the
locative₂:

```
[an]-ne₂ nam dug₃ mu-ri-in-tar
'The god An has determined a good destiny for you.'
Ninazu A 26 — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 462   (2.SG in the locative₂)
```

But when a locative₁ is also needed in the same verb, the same participant appears in the
dative instead:

```
ubur₂ kug-ŋu₁₀-a nam ma-ra-ni-tar
'I have decided your fate with my holy bosom.'
Shulgi P Segment C 25 — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 463   (2.SG now in the dative)
```

So a compound verb's "normal" case frame can be overridden by pressure elsewhere in the verb.
The frames listed in §8 are the default, not an absolute rule.

### An open question you should know about

For **shu—ti**, the two standard reference grammars analyse the word _shu_ differently.
Jagersma treats it as an **indirect object in the directive case** (2010: §17.3 ex. 73);
Zólyomi treats it as a nominal element in the **locative₃** (2017: §15.2). The translation is
the same either way, and so is what you see on the tablet — the ending `-e` on _shu_. Only
the label differs. Where the specialists disagree, a learner is entitled to note the
disagreement and move on.

## 5. Conjugating a compound verb

Good news: **only the verb inflects.** The noun never takes tense, aspect, person or number
marking. Everything you have learned about the Sumerian verb applies unchanged; the noun
simply sits in front, inert.

Sumerian verbs distinguish two aspects\*, traditionally called by their Akkadian names
**ḫamṭu\*** (perfective — a complete, bounded event, usually translated with a past tense)
and **marû\*** (imperfective — an ongoing, habitual or future event). In a compound verb,
that distinction lives entirely in the verbal half.

Here is one clause from an Ur III letter, in two of its attested spellings:

```
Imperfective (marû):
  kishib-ba-ne₂  shu ha-ba-shi-ib₂-ti-ĝe₂₆
  kishib=ane=ø   shu=e  ha=ø-ba-n-shi-b-te.ĝ-e
                        MOD-VP-3N.IO-3SG-to-3N.DO-approach-3SG.A:IPFV

  'He should receive his sealed document from him.'
  TCS 1:52 8 (Umma, 21st c. BCE)

The same form, abbreviated by a different scribe:
  kishib-ba-ne₂  shu ha-ba-shi-ib₂-ti
  TCS 1:162 7 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE)
```

— Jagersma 2010: §12.3 ex. 13a–b

And a second pair, with **shu—bala** 'to alter':

```
di ku₅-a-ĝa₂            shu ni-ib₂-bala-e-a
di.d kud-ø-'a=ĝu='a     shu=ø  ø-ni-b-bala-e-'a
                               VP-in-3N.DO-cross-3SG.A:IPFV-NOM

'the one who will alter the judgement passed by me'
(lit. 'who will let his hand go across in it')
Gudea Statue B 8:17–18 — Jagersma 2010: §12.2 ex. 1
```

_bala-e_ is the imperfective. The _shu_ has not changed at all.

### The three ways a verb builds its imperfective

Jagersma (2010: §12.3) sorts Sumerian verbs into three groups. Which group a verb belongs to
is unpredictable and simply has to be memorised — which is why the reference list in §7 gives
both stems for every entry.

**1. No special stem** — the large majority. The imperfective is marked only by the endings
(`-e`, `-ed` and the imperfective person-suffixes). _bar_ → _bar-re_; _aĝ₂_ → _aĝ₂-e_.

**2. A reduplicated\* stem** — the verb repeats part of itself in the imperfective.
Jagersma names this group: **ĝar, gi₄, gur₁₀, ha-la, kara₂, kiĝ₂, ku₄(r), mu₂, naĝ, niĝin₂,
ra, sa₁₀, su(g), taka₄, te(n), tu₅, tuku, tuku₅, zig₃** — adding that the list "is certainly
not exhaustive." So _ĝar_ → **ĝa₂-ĝa₂**, _gi₄_ → **gi₄-gi₄**, _zig₃_ → **zi-zi**.

**3. A suppletive\* stem** — a completely different word steps in, like English _go_ / _went_.
The ones you will actually meet: **dug₄ → e** (with _di_ in non-finite forms), **ĝen → du**
'to go', **ře₆ → tum₂** 'to bring', **te → te(ĝ)** 'to approach', and, for 'to sit',
**tush → dur₂** (Zólyomi 2017: Table 10.1).

### Two warnings about what you see on the tablet

These are the traps, and they catch experienced readers too.

**A doubled sign is ambiguous.** Sumerian uses _full_ reduplication to mark verbal plurality
(many events) and _partial_ reduplication to mark the imperfective. The writing system
normally spells both by simply writing the sign twice. Jagersma's example: Ur III messenger
texts often write _dab₅-dab₅-de₃_ 'in order to seize (runaways)', which looks like an
imperfective stem — but the many _non_-reduplicated imperfectives of _dab₅_ elsewhere prove
it is really a plural (2010: §12.3).

**Scribes both over- and under-write.** One and the same verbal form is attested three ways
in Ur III letters: _ti-ĝe₂₆_ (written out phonetically), _ti_ (abbreviated, giving no hint
that a special stem is involved), and _TI.TI_ (a reduplication that does not exist in the
spoken form at all) — Jagersma 2010: §12.3 ex. 13a–c. **Never read stem shape straight off
the signs.** Read it off the grammar and the context.

## 6. Which nouns build compound verbs?

Overwhelmingly, **body parts**. The group is large enough that Fumi Karahashi devoted an
entire doctoral thesis to it: _Sumerian Compound Verbs with Body-Part Terms_ (Chicago, 2000).

Counting the multi-word verbal entries in the ePSD2 lexicon — 723 of them — and ranking by
how many distinct compound verbs each noun forms:

| Noun                     | Gloss                | Distinct compounds | Examples                                    |
| ------------------------ | -------------------- | -----------------: | ------------------------------------------- |
| **shu** 𒋗                | hand                 |                 73 | shu—ti, shu—bar, shu—ĝar, shu—bala, shu—tag |
| **a / a₂** 𒀀 / 𒀉         | water / arm, power   |                 42 | a—ru, a—de₂, a₂—aĝ₂                         |
| **gu₂ / gu₃** 𒄘 / 𒅗      | neck / voice         |                 35 | gu₂—ĝar, gu₂—la₂, gu₃—de₂, gu₃—ra           |
| **saĝ** 𒊕                | head                 |                 30 | saĝ—il₂, saĝ—rig₇                           |
| **igi** 𒅆                | eye, face            |                 29 | igi—bar, igi—du₈, igi—ĝal₂, igi—kar₂        |
| **ĝesh** 𒄑               | wood, tree           |                 18 | ĝesh—tuku, ĝesh—tag, ĝesh—ra                |
| **shag₄** 𒊮              | heart, interior      |                 17 | shag₄—hul₂, shag₄—kush₂, shag₄—dug₃         |
| **ĝiri₃** 𒄊              | foot                 |                 15 | ĝiri₃—gub                                   |
| **ki** 𒆠                 | place, ground        |                 15 | ki—aĝ₂, ki—ĝar, ki—us₂                      |
| **ni₂** 𒉎                | self, fear           |                 15 | ni₂—te(ĝ₃)                                  |
| **inim** 𒅗               | word                 |                 14 | inim—ĝar, inim—gi₄, inim—bala               |
| **dub / zu₂ / ĝeshtug₂** | tablet / tooth / ear |      11 / 10 / ~10 | zu₂—keshe₂, ĝeshtug₂—ĝar                    |

Count instead by how often the forms actually occur in texts, and **shu** stands entirely
alone — over twenty thousand attestations, the great bulk of them the single compound
**shu—ti** 'to receive', appearing on receipt after receipt throughout the Ur III archives.
If you learn one compound verb today, learn that one.

On the other side of the pairing, the most productive verbal elements are **dug₄** (45
distinct compounds), **du / du₇ / du₈** (29), **ĝar** (28), **la₂** (24), **gi₄** (23),
**ĝal₂** (20), **ak** (20), **sig₁₀** (19) and **e** (18).

Three other families are worth naming:

- **Abstract nouns in _nam-_**: nam—tar 'to decree fate', nam—kud 'to curse',
  nam-erim₂—kud 'to swear an oath', nam-lugal—ak 'to reign as king'.
- **Ordinary concrete nouns verbalised with _ak_**: ĝesh-ga-rig₂—ak 'to comb',
  en-nu-uĝ₃—ak 'to guard', kiĝ₂—ak 'to work'.
- **Ideophones\*** — noise-words — construed with the verb **za** 'to make the noise'.
  Jagersma cites _bu-ud-ba-ad—za_ 'to clatter' (2010: §12.2, cf. §4.5.4). ePSD2 records a
  whole family of these built on the verb **sha₄**: _ad—sha₄_ 'to resound',
  _she—sha₄_ 'to moan', _shud₃—sha₄_ 'to pray'.

## 7. Compound verbs give back: how they make nouns

The story does not end with verbs. Put a compound verb into its non-finite\* (participial)
form and you get a noun — which is why so many Sumerian job titles look like frozen
sentences.

- **shu-bala** 'change', **shu-gid₂** 'selection', **shu-luh** 'the lustration ritual',
  **shu-tag** 'decoration' — all straight from the compound verbs of the same shape
  (Jagersma 2010: §6.5.4).
- **igi-nu-du₈** — a class of low-status orchard worker. Literally 'a not-eye-opening one',
  i.e. 'blind person', built on the negated participle of _igi—du₈_ 'to see'.
- **mash-shu-gid₂-gid₂** 'diviner' — 'one who stretches out (_gid₂_) the hands (_shu_) into
  the kids (_mash_)', i.e. a reader of goat entrails. Jagersma spots a neat proof that this
  is now a genuine single _word_ and no longer a phrase: the compound verb _shu—gid₂_
  normally requires a locative ending on its object, and that ending is conspicuously
  **missing** after _mash_ (2010: §6.5.4).
- **shu-tag—dug₄** 'to decorate' — a compound verb whose _noun_ is itself the participle of
  another compound verb (Zólyomi 2017: Lesson 7). Sumerian recycling at its finest.

## 8. Reference list: 50 compound verbs

**How to read the table.** _PFV_ is the perfective (ḫamṭu) stem; _IPFV_ the imperfective
(marû) stem — see §5. In the IPFV column:

- **= PFV** means the verb has no special imperfective stem; you build the imperfective from
  the same stem plus the imperfective endings.
- A reduplicated form is given where the verb belongs to Jagersma's reduplication class.
- **(supplet.)** marks a suppletive stem — a different word entirely.
- **(?)** marks a stem where the evidence is thin, or where the reduplicated spellings could
  equally well be plurals. Treat those with caution.

The last column, **Case**, gives the case the _semantic object_ takes — the participant the
action is really about (see §4). **An empty cell does not mean the verb has no frame**; it
means no frame is securely established in the sources used for this page, and you should
check ePSD2 or Attinger before assuming one. Where two cases are given, the first is the
default and the second an attested alternative — usually the human counterpart of a
non-human locative, or a dative promotion.

Cuneiform is the standard Ur III / Old Babylonian writing of the pair.

## Notes on terminology

Terms marked with an asterisk\* in the text, in the order they appear.

**non-referential** — A noun is _referential_ when it points to something in the world:
in _he ate the bread_, "the bread" is a real loaf. A non-referential noun points to nothing;
it has been absorbed into the meaning of the verb. The _igi_ 'eye' of _igi—bar_ 'to look at'
is non-referential, which is why you cannot count it, own it or describe it.

**ergative (ERG)** — The case marking the subject of a transitive verb (the one who does
something to something else). Sumerian marks it with `-e`. English does not have this: we say
_he saw_ and _he slept_ alike, but Sumerian marks the _he_ of _he saw_ differently.

**absolutive (ABS)** — The case marking both the direct object of a transitive verb and the
subject of an intransitive one. Sumerian marks it with nothing at all — a zero ending, which
we write `ø`. In a compound verb, the noun normally sits in the absolutive.

**terminative (TERM)** — The case meaning 'to, towards', marked `-she₃`.

**comitative (COM)** — The case meaning 'with, together with', marked `-da`.

**dative (DAT)** — The case meaning 'to, for' with _persons_, marked `-ra`.

**directive (DIR) / locative₃** — The case marked `-e`, indicating something the action is
directed at or applied to. Older grammars call it _locative-terminative_; you will meet all
three names. It is the commonest frame for the semantic object of a compound verb.

**locative₂** — Another of the three cases Sumerian marks in the same slot of the verb,
roughly 'in, on', with the ending `-'a`. Grammars number the locatives differently, which is
why you see subscripts.

**morphemic analysis / glossing** — Breaking a written word into its smallest meaningful
pieces (_morphemes_) and labelling each one. It is how Sumerologists show their working.

**light verb** — A verb that has been drained of its own meaning and serves mainly to turn
a noun into a predicate. English _take_ in _take a walk_, _make_ in _make a decision_.
Sumerian's two are _ak_ 'do, make' and _dug₄_ 'do, say'.

**indirect object** — The participant something is given, said or done _to_, as distinct from
the thing given (_he gave **her** the tablet_). Sumerian marks it inside the verb with a
dedicated prefix.

**dative promotion** — Sumerian verbs have a single slot for a locative prefix. When a clause
would need two locatives at once, one of the participants is re-cast as an indirect object and
marked with the dative instead. The result is that a verb's usual case frame can be
temporarily overridden.

**aspect** — Not the same as tense. Tense says _when_; aspect says what _shape_ the event has
— complete and bounded, or ongoing and unbounded. Sumerian grammaticalises aspect, not tense.

**ḫamṭu** — Akkadian for 'quick'. The Babylonian scribes' name for the Sumerian **perfective**
aspect: a complete, bounded event, most often translated with an English past tense
(_he built it_). This is the form given in dictionaries.

**marû** — Akkadian for 'slow, fat'. The Babylonian scribes' name for the Sumerian
**imperfective** aspect: an ongoing, habitual or future event (_he is building it_, _he will
be building it_). Some scholars prefer to treat the ḫamṭu/marû contrast as past versus
present-future tense rather than aspect; the debate is live, and the Akkadian labels are used
precisely because they are neutral between the two views.

**non-finite (NFIN)** — A verb form that is not marked for a subject — the equivalent of an
English infinitive or participle (_to build_, _building_, _built_). Non-finite forms of
compound verbs are a major source of Sumerian nouns; see §7.

**nominalising suffix (NOM)** — The ending `-'a` (written _-a_), which turns a clause into
something a noun phrase can contain: 'the field **which he irrigated**'.

**reduplication** — Repeating a word or part of it. Sumerian uses _full_ reduplication for
verbal plurality (many events) and _partial_ reduplication to build some imperfective stems.
The writing system spells both the same way, which is a standing source of ambiguity.

**suppletive** — Said of a paradigm where the forms come from historically unrelated words,
like English _go_ / _went_ or _good_ / _better_. Sumerian _dug₄_ / _e_ and _ĝen_ / _du_ are
suppletive pairs.

**ideophone** — A word that depicts a sound or sensation directly, like English _clang_ or
_whoosh_. Sumerian ideophones are made into verbs with _za_ 'to make the noise'.

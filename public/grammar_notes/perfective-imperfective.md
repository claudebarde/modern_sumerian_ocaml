# Perfective and imperfective

> **A note on spelling.** This page uses the Modern Sumerian writing conventions:
> **š → sh**, **ḫ → h**, **ŋ → ĝ**. So *ŋar* appears here as **ĝar**, *šum₂* as **shum₂**,
> *zaḫ₃* as **zah₃**. The two Akkadian technical terms **ḫamṭu** and **marû** keep their
> traditional spelling, since they are not Sumerian words. Examples quoted from the reference
> grammars have been re-spelled to match and their transliterations normalised to the
> index-number style (*é* → **e₂**, *ĝír* → **ĝir₂**).

---

## 1. The question Sumerian does not answer

Every time you use an English verb, the grammar forces you to say **when**. You cannot say
*he build* — you must choose *builds*, *built*, *will build*, *was building*, *has built*.
English makes you place the event on a timeline before you are allowed to say anything else.

Sumerian does not ask that question. It asks a different one, and it is the question that
gives this page its title:

> **Is this event complete and bounded, or is it still running?**

A Sumerian finite verb comes in one of two shapes. One shape presents the event as a whole —
finished, sealed, viewed from outside. The other presents it as unfinished — in progress,
habitual, still to come. Neither shape says anything directly about past or future. Context
does that.

The practical consequence is the single most important thing on this page:

> **A "perfective" Sumerian verb is not a past tense, and an "imperfective" one is not a
> present or future tense.** Most of the time they translate that way. But the mismatches
> are frequent enough, and systematic enough, that translating by reflex will mislead you.

We will get to the mismatches in §7. First, what the two forms are called, and why the
naming is such a mess.

## 2. Three sets of names for two forms

You will meet these forms under at least three different pairs of labels, depending on which
book you have open. All three describe the same two things.

| | The complete form | The incomplete form |
|---|---|---|
| Babylonian scribes, revived by Yoshikawa 1968 | **ḫamṭu\*** ('quick') | **marû\*** ('slow, fat') |
| Poebel 1934, Falkenstein 1949, Zólyomi 2017 | **preterite (PT)** | **present-future (PF)** |
| Diakonoff 1967, Jagersma 2010, most current work | **perfective (PFV)** | **imperfective (IPFV)** |

The history is worth knowing, because it explains why the labels conflict.

**The tense era.** The founders of the field described Sumerian as having tenses. Poebel
(1934) called them 'preterite' and 'present-future'; Falkenstein (1949, 1950) used the German
equivalents. Falkenstein went further and held that only the *transitive* verb had two
tenses, the intransitive having a single *Normalform* for both. His view dominated for half
a century.

**The aspect turn.** Two things broke it. Diakonoff (1967) proposed an aspect system, but
wrote in Russian and had little immediate reach. Independently, Yoshikawa (1968) revived
the Old Babylonian grammatical terms *ḫamṭu* and *marû* and argued the contrast was aspectual.
Jagersma summarises where that left the field:

> "That Sumerian has a system of tenses is a view now largely abandoned. And with good
> reason. It is not difficult to find 'preterit' forms with present or future reference, or
> 'present-future' forms with past reference."
> — Jagersma 2010: §15.1

**But the argument is not over.** Zólyomi's 2017 textbook — the other grammar we use on this
site — still uses *preterite* and *present-future*, and defends a tense analysis, though a
subtler one than Falkenstein's:

> "Sumerian has a relative tense system: the verbal form called here the present-future
> denotes actions that are not anterior, but simultaneous or posterior, relative to a given
> reference point. The verbal form called the preterite denotes actions anterior relative to
> a given reference point."
> — Zólyomi 2017: §9.1

That is a real difference, not just vocabulary. A **relative tense\*** system locates the
event against a reference point that may itself sit anywhere in time — which is how Zólyomi
accommodates the "present-future" forms that describe the past. An **aspect** system says
the contrast is not about time at all.

**On this site we use *perfective* and *imperfective***, following Jagersma and the majority
of current work. But when you open Zólyomi you will see PT and PF, and when you open older
literature or a dictionary you will see ḫamṭu and marû. They line up exactly as the table
above shows.

One reason for the persistent uncertainty is worth stating plainly:

> "In spite of this discussion about 'tense or aspect', there has been very little research
> into the actual uses and meanings of the two sets of forms. It is for this reason that many
> Sumerologists remain quite reluctant to make a definite statement on the issue."
> — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.1

## 3. The rule that anchors everything

Before any of the machinery, learn this one fact. It is the most reliable statement anyone
has made about the Sumerian verb, and it settles a large proportion of real cases:

> **States are always perfective.**

The observation is Krecher's (1995: 143), and Jagersma builds his account around it:

> "States are always expressed with perfective forms. Verbs which can only have a stative
> meaning (*me* 'be', *ĝal₂* 'be (somewhere)') are only found in the perfective. Other verbs,
> which can have a stative meaning in the perfective, always show a dynamic (non-stative)
> meaning in the imperfective."
> — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2

Two consequences follow, and both are useful:

**1. Two very common verbs have no imperfective at all.** *me* 'to be' and *ĝal₂* 'to be
there, exist' are perfective-only. If you are composing Sumerian and reach for an imperfective
*ĝal₂*, stop — there isn't one.

**2. Putting a stative verb in the imperfective changes its meaning, not just its aspect.**
It becomes dynamic — usually causative or inchoative:

```
ama dba-u₂   en dnin-ĝir₂-su-da     ki-nu₂    mu-da-ab-du₁₀-ge
ama bau=e    en ninĝirsuk=da        kinu=ø    ø-mu-n-da-b-dug-e
mother Bau=ERG lord Ningirsu=COM    bed=ABS   VP-VEN-3SG-with-3N.DO-be.sweet-3SG.A:IPFV

'Mother Bau makes the bed sweet with lord Ningirsu.'
Gudea Cylinder B 17:2–3 (Lagash, 22nd c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 79
```

*dug₃* is a stative verb, 'be sweet, be good'. In the imperfective it cannot mean 'is sweet';
it means 'makes sweet'. Two more of the same kind:

```
nam-mah-a-ne₂     kalam-e       he₂-zu-zu
nammah=ane=ø      kalam=e       ha='i-zu~zu-e
greatness=his=ABS country=ERG   MOD=VP-know:IPFV-3SG.A:IPFV

'May the country come to know his greatness!'   (not 'may it know')
Gudea Statue B 9:29–30 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 80
```

```
nin-gu-la   dam shag₄-ga-na-ke₄        ha-ba-tuku-tuku
ningula=ø   [dam shag=ane=ak]=e        ha=ø-ba-n-tuku~tuku-e
PN=ABS      husband heart=her=GEN=ERG  MOD=VP-MM-3SG.DO-have:IPFV-3SG.A:IPFV

'Let Ningula be married by a husband of her own choice!'
SRU 85 rev. 12'–14' (Isin, 23rd c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 81
```

*tuku* 'have' is stative. The imperfective *tuku-tuku* is the dynamic 'come to have, take
(in marriage)'.

**Why this is genuinely surprising.** In Russian and most European aspect languages, stative
meaning belongs to the *imperfective*. Sumerian is the mirror image. Jagersma flags the
implication as "huge" — it means Sumerian aspect is not simply the Slavic system under
another name, and it is one of the reasons the tense-versus-aspect question stays open
(2010: §15.4.1, citing Forsyth 1970 and Comrie 1976).

## 4. How the two forms are built

Sumerian marks the contrast in **three** ways, and a given verb may use one, two or all
three. Learn to check all three, because on any single verb one of them may be invisible.

> **How to read the glossed examples on this page.** Line 1 is the transliteration as written
> on the tablet. Line 2 is the morphemic\* analysis, with `=` marking a clitic boundary and
> `-` an affix boundary. Line 3 labels each block; `ø` is an element present in the grammar
> but written with no sign. Following Jagersma's convention, **only imperfective forms carry
> an explicit `IPFV` gloss** — anything unmarked is perfective.

### Mechanism 1: different person marking

This is the big one for transitive verbs, and it is the reason §5 of the
[noun cases](/learn/grammar_notes/noun-cases) page said Sumerian is *split-ergative*. The two forms use different agreement
systems.

**Perfective inflection** — ergative. A prefix marks the transitive subject (A); one set of
suffixes marks both the direct object (P) and the intransitive subject (S).

**Imperfective inflection** — partly accusative, partly tripartite. A *suffix* marks the
subject; a *prefix* marks the direct object.

Put crudely, and it is worth memorising in this crude form:

> **Perfective: agent in front of the stem. Imperfective: agent behind the stem.**

Here is Jagersma's own minimal pair — same dialect, same city, near-identical construction,
one generation apart:

```
Perfective:
  be₂-ĝar-re-esh
  ø-bi-n-ĝar-esh
  VP-3N:on-3SG.A-place-3PL
  'they placed it (= silver) on it (= sheep)'
  Uru-inimgina 4 4:1 (Lagash, 24th c. BCE)

Imperfective:
  e-ĝa₂-ĝa₂-ne
  'i-b(i)-ĝar~ĝar-enē
  VP-3N:on-place:IPFV-3PL.A:IPFV
  'they were placing it (= silver) on it (= sheep)'
  Uru-inimgina 6 1:21' (Lagash, 24th c. BCE)
```

— Jagersma 2010: §15.2.1 exx. 1–2

Both differences are visible here at once: the stem changes (*ĝar* → *ĝa₂-ĝa₂*) and so does
the person marking (*-n-* before the stem versus *-enē* after it).

The full agreement paradigm (Jagersma 2010: §15.2.1):

| | **Perfective** | | | **Imperfective** | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Transitive* | Subj. | Stem | Obj. | Obj. | Stem | Subj. |
| 1 sg. | ' | | -en | ' | | -en |
| 2 sg. | e | | -en | e | | -en |
| 3 sg. human | n | | -ø | n | | -e |
| 3 non-human | b | | -ø | (b) | | -e |
| 1 pl. | | | -enden | | | -enden |
| 2 pl. | | | -enzen | | | -enzen |
| 3 pl. human | | | -esh | | | -enē |

| *Intransitive* | **Perfective** | **Imperfective** |
|---|---|---|
| 1 sg. | -en | -ed-en |
| 2 sg. | -en | -ed-en |
| 3 sg. / non-human | -ø | -ed-ø |
| 1 pl. | -enden | -ed-enden |
| 2 pl. | -enzen | -ed-enzen |
| 3 pl. human | -esh | -ed-esh |

Notice the transitive third person. In the perfective the third-person object suffix is
**-ø** (nothing); in the imperfective the third-person *subject* suffix is **-e**. That
little *-e* on the end of a verb is very often the only visible signal that a form is
imperfective.

### Mechanism 2: a different stem

Some verbs — a minority, but a very frequently used minority — change shape. Which verbs do
this is not predictable:

> "Which verb has such a stem and which not is completely unpredictable. […] The presence or
> absence of such a special stem seems to be the result of a historical development which can
> not be traced anymore."
> — Jagersma 2010: §12.3

The four classes are set out in §5 below.

### Mechanism 3: the suffix {ed}

Here is the elegant part of the system. For *intransitive* verbs, mechanism 1 does nothing —
the perfective and imperfective use exactly the same subject suffixes. And most verbs have no
special stem, so mechanism 2 does nothing either. Something has to carry the distinction, and
that something is the suffix **{ed}**.

> "A stem modification that applies to all verbs alike is the use of the suffix {ed}. This
> suffix is used in all non-finite and nearly all intransitive finite imperfective forms. In
> other words, it occurs in exactly those imperfective forms that would otherwise lack a
> consistent formal distinction with the perfective."
> — Jagersma 2010: §15.3.1

```
u₄ geme₂-dlama₃      ba-ug₇-e-da-a
u₄.d gemelama=ø      ba-ug-ed-ø-'a='a
day PN=ABS           MM-die:IPFV-IPFV-3SG.S:IPFV-NOM=LOC

'when Geme-Lama dies'
NG 7 15 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.2.3 ex. 9
```

```
u₄ a-ra₂ 3-ka        i₃-zah₃-de₃-na
u₄.d a.ra 3=ak='a    'i-zah-ed-en-'a='a
day time 3=GEN=LOC   VP-run.away-IPFV-1SG.S:IPFV-NOM=LOC

'on the day when I will run away for the third time'
NRVN 1:1 6–7 (Nippur, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.2.3 ex. 11
```

Two spelling notes that will save you time. The `/d/` of {ed} is **never written in
syllable-final position** — so `ĝesh e-tag-ge` is *tag-ed-ø*, with the *d* invisible. And the
`/e/` of {ed} becomes `/u/` after stems with the vowel `/u/`: *shum₂-mu-da* is
*shum-ed-ø-'a*, not a reduplication (Jagersma 2010: §15.3.2).

**One exception**: the verb *du* 'go, come' never takes {ed}, in finite or non-finite forms.

### The three mechanisms, graded

Zólyomi's Lesson 9 gives a beautifully graded set of three verb pairs — one mechanism, then
two, then all three:

```
(a) AGREEMENT ONLY — the verb shum₂ 'give', same stem both times

    Perfective:    ma-an-shum₂            'Enlil has given Gutium to me.'
                   m-a-n-shum-ø            (Utu-heĝal 29)
                   VEN-DAT-3SG.A-give-3SG.P

    Imperfective:  im-ma-shum₂-mu          'Gudea entrusted the statue with this message.'
                   'i-m-b-a-shum-e         (Gudea Statue B 7:21–23)
                   FIN-VEN-3N-DAT-give-3SG.A

(b) AGREEMENT + STEM — the verb gi₄ 'return'

    Perfective:    i₃-ni-gi₄               'He let the child return to his mother.'
                   'i-nn-i-n-gi-ø           (En-metena 4 4:3)

    Imperfective:  bi₂-ib₂-gi₄-gi₄-a       'the ruler who revokes these (offerings)'
                   b-i-b-gi~gi-e-'a         (Gudea Statue B 1:13–14)

(c) AGREEMENT + STEM + {ed} — gi₄ again, this time intransitive

    Imperfective:  ba-a-gi₄-gi₄-da         '… who revokes my gifts …'
                   ba-e-gi~gi-ed-ø-'a       (Gudea Statue B 8:19–20)
```

— Zólyomi 2017: §9.1, exx. 165–169

## 5. The four stem classes

Zólyomi sorts Sumerian verbs into four groups by what they do in the imperfective
(2017: §9.3). This is the most usable classification, so we follow it here.

### i. Regular class — no change

**The great majority of verbs.** The stem is identical in both aspects; only the agreement
(and, intransitively, {ed}) tells them apart. *shum₂*, *du₃*, *ak*, *tag*, *bala*, *gaz*,
*la₂*, *tar*, *hul₂* and hundreds more.

### ii. Reduplicating class

The imperfective stem repeats part of the perfective stem. Crucially, this is a **partial**
reduplication, not the full reduplication that marks plural events (see §8). Zólyomi describes
three patterns (2017: §9.3):

- **Stem ends in a consonant, consonant dropped**: *ĝar* → *ĝa₂-ĝa₂* /ĝa~ĝa/;
  *naĝ* → *na₈-na₈* /na~na/; *ku₄(r)* → *ku₄-ku₄* /ku~ku/
- **Stem ends in a consonant, consonant kept in the first syllable only**:
  *hal* → *hal-ha* /hal~ha/; *te-en* → *te-en-te* /ten~te/
- **Monosyllabic stem ending in a vowel, whole stem repeated**: *gi₄* → *gi₄-gi₄* /gi~gi/;
  *mu₂* → *mu₂-mu₂* /mu~mu/
- **Some disyllabic verbs reduplicate the first syllable and voice the consonant**:
  *tuku* → *du₁₂-du₁₂* /du~du/; *taka₄* → *da₁₃-da₁₃* /da~da/

Jagersma names the members he is confident of (2010: §12.3): **ĝar, gi₄, gur₁₀, ha-la, kara₂,
kiĝ₂, ku₄(r), mu₂, naĝ, niĝin₂, ra, sa₁₀, su(g), taka₄, te(n), tu₅, tuku, tuku₅, zig₃** —
adding that the list "is certainly not exhaustive."

He also notes that the reduplicated stem always ends in a vowel, which we know because the
person suffixes always take their post-vocalic forms after one.

### iii. Extending class

The stem gains a final consonant in the imperfective. Only two members, and both are common:

- *e₃* 'go out' → *ed₂* — perfective /e/, imperfective /ed/
- *te* or *ti* 'approach' → *teĝ₃* or *tiĝ₃* — perfective /te/, imperfective /teĝ/

The added consonant only shows in the writing if something follows the stem — which is why
you see *ti-ĝe₂₆* in a full spelling and bare *ti* in an abbreviated one (Zólyomi 2017: §9.3).

> **A disagreement here.** Zólyomi puts *e₃* in the extending class. Jagersma reads the
> attested writing È.È as a reduplicated imperfective stem instead — e.g.
> *lu₂ e₂-ninnu-ta im-ta-ab-È.È-a* 'the man who will remove it from the Eninnu'
> (Gudea Statue B 8:6–7), glossed `go.out:IPFV`. The form is the same on the tablet; the
> analysis differs.

### iv. Suppletive class

A different word entirely, like English *go / went*:

| Perfective | Imperfective | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **dug₄** | **e** (participle **di**) | say, do |
| **ĝen** | **du** | go, come (singular) |
| **er** | **su₈-b** | go, come (plural) |
| **de₆ / re₆** | **tum₂** | bring |
| **tush** | **dur₂** | sit |

(Jagersma 2010: §12.3; Zólyomi 2017: Table 10.1)

Note that several of these verbs are doing **two** jobs at once — they also alternate for
verbal number. *dug₄* is singular-perfective while *e* is both plural-perfective and
imperfective; *ĝen* is singular and *er* plural. Jagersma sets out the whole set in §12.4.2.

### One verb that breaks the rules

The imperfective *tum₂* 'bring' takes **perfective** agreement in its transitive forms. It is
not alone in mixing the two: imperative forms and forms with the modal prefix {ga} both use
perfective *stems* with imperfective *agreement*. Jagersma lists all three cases and adds:
"An explanation for these irregularities is still lacking" (2010: §15.3.1).

## 6. Translating the perfective

Here is where the reflex "perfective = past" earns its keep, and where it fails.

### Past action — the default

```
ki-ba          na          bi₂-ru₂
ki=be='a       na=ø        ø-bi-n-ru-ø
place=this=LOC stone=ABS   VP-3N:on-3SG.A-erect-3N

'He erected a stela on this place.'
En-metena 28 1:12 (Lagash, 25th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 73
```

### Present state

```
e₂-ba           sam₂-be₂        nu-til
[e=be]=ak       sam=be=ø        nu='i-til-ø
house=this=GEN  price=its=ABS   NEG=VP-end-3N

'The price of this house is not paid in full.'
NG 104 8 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 64
```

Present tense in English, perfective in Sumerian, because it is a **state**.

### Future state

```
lugal-ĝu₁₀     he₂-en-zu
lugal=ĝu=e     ha='i-n-zu-ø
king=my=ERG    MOD=VP-3SG.A-know-3N

'May my king know it!'
TMHC NF 4:42 31 (21st c. BCE, OB copy) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 66
```

### Timeless truth

```
igi hush-a-ĝu₁₀             kur-re          nu-um-il₂
[igi hush-ø-'a]=ĝu=ø        kur=e           nu='i-m-il-ø
eye be.angry-NFIN-NOM=my=ABS mountains=ERG  NEG=VP-VEN-lift-3N

'The mountain lands cannot bear my angry look.'
Gudea Cylinder A 9:25 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 71
```

```
a₂ bar-a-ĝu₁₀    lu₂    la-ba-ta-e₃
'No man escapes from the reach of my arms.'
Gudea Cylinder A 9:26 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 72
```

Gnomic present in English, perfective in Sumerian. The perfective is "the form used to
express a timeless truth" (Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2, citing Alster 2005: 209).

### Stative passive

```
ĝesh-u₃-shub kug            si    ib₂-sa₂
[ushub kug]=ø               si=e  'i-b-sa-ø
brick.mould pure=ABS        ?=DIR VP-3N.OO-be.equal-3N

'A holy brick mould was readied (= stood ready).'
Gudea Cylinder A 5:6 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 68
```

### Two places where the perfective is compulsory

**After the relative-past prefix {u₃}**, regardless of time reference:

```
nam-lu₂-ulu₃ iri-na / shu u₃-na-zi / shag₄ iri-na-ka / ha-ne₂-gaz-e
'May the population, having raised their hands against him in his city, kill him in the
 middle of his city!'
En-metena 28 6:26–29 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 76
```

The first verb (*u₃-na-zi*) is perfective because {u₃} requires it; the second (*ha-ne₂-gaz-e*)
is imperfective.

**In conditional clauses with *tukum-be₂* 'if':**

```
tukum-be₂ / nu-na-an-shum₂ / e₂-a-ne₂-ta / ib₂-su-su
'If he does not give this to him, he shall repay this out of his estate.'
TCS 1:177 8–11 (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 77
```

Perfective in the *if*-clause, imperfective in the *then*-clause. (But conditional clauses
with *u₄-da* regularly take imperfectives instead.)

### Verbs of speaking — the direct-speech rule

This one is mechanical and very handy:

> **If the verb of speaking comes *after* the quoted words and describes a past act, it is
> perfective.** If it comes *before* the quotation, or is not past, it is imperfective.

```
na-gu lu₂-sukkal-ke₄ ma-an-bala   bi₂-in-du₁₁
'Nagu said: "Lusukkal transferred it to me".'
NG 145 8' (Lagash, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.2 ex. 78
```

The logic is aspectual: a speech act quoted before its verb has already reached its endpoint.

## 7. Translating the imperfective

The one absolute: **the imperfective never expresses a state.** Everything else is a matter
of context.

### Present ongoing action

```
a dug₃      e-na-de₂-e
a dug=ø     'i-nna-de-e
water sweet=ABS VP-3SG.IO-pour-3SG.A:IPFV

'He pours fresh water for him.'
Lugal-zagesi 1 (Uruk, 24th c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 83
```

```
nidba gal-gal          e-na-gid₂-de₃
'He increases the main food offerings for him.'
Lugal-zagesi 1 3:10–11 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 82
```

### Future

```
e₂ ud-de₃ ma-ra-du₃-e,            ĝi₆-e ma-ra-ab-mu₂-mu₂
e=ø ud=e mu-r-a-du-e              ĝi=e mu-r-a-b-mu~mu-e
house=ABS day=ERG VEN-2SG-DAT-build-3SG.A   night=ERG VEN-2SG-DAT-3N.P-grow:IPFV-3SG.A

'The day will build the temple for you and the night will make it rise for you.'
Gudea Cylinder A 12:1–2 — Zólyomi 2017: §9.3 ex. 176
```

Note *du₃* (regular class, no stem change) and *mu₂* (reduplicating class) side by side in one
sentence — a good illustration that class membership is lexical, not grammatical.

### Past progressive

This is the case that breaks the tense reflex hardest:

```
kur nu₁₁-ta               nu₁₁         mu-na-ta-e₁₁-de₃
[kur nu=ak]=ta            nu=ø         ø-mu-nna-ta-e.d-ed-ø
alabaster.mountains=ABL   alabaster=ABS VP-VEN-3SG.IO-from-descend-IPFV-3N.S:IPFV

'From the alabaster mountains alabaster was coming down for him.'
Gudea Cylinder A 16:24 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 91
```

```
sipad-de₃    e₂ kug-ga     mu-ru₂-e
'The shepherd was building the temple with silver.'
Gudea Cylinder A 16:25 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 93
```

An English past progressive throughout, and Sumerian imperfective throughout. Falkenstein
treated these as a literary flourish; on the aspect analysis they are exactly what the
grammar predicts.

### Simultaneity — an action running alongside another

```
dub mul-an dug₃-ga im-mi-ĝal₂ /   ad im-da-ab-gi₄-gi₄
                                  ad=ø 'i-m-da-b-gi~gi-e

'She had a star tablet on her knee and was consulting it.'
Gudea Cylinder A 4:26–5:1 — Jagersma 2010: §15.4.3 ex. 94
```

The first verb is perfective (*ĝal₂*, a state — and a verb that has no imperfective at all);
the second is imperfective, because the consulting was going on at the same time. This
pairing — perfective for the frame, imperfective for what runs inside it — is a standard
narrative device.

### Deontic 'shall' — the language of contracts

Very common in legal and administrative texts, and the reason imperfectives are everywhere in
the Ur III archives:

```
i₃-na-ze₂-er-e / ba-ab-tum₂-mu
'Inazer will take it (= a donkey) away.'
BPOA 1:1285 9–10 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.2.3 ex. 6

kishib lu₂ nu-ub-da-su-su-da-ne
'sealed documents of people with whom they (= the debts) will not be repaid'
Nebraska 19 52 (Umma, 21st c. BCE) — Jagersma 2010: §15.3.3 ex. 54
```

### A translation cheat-sheet

| Sumerian form | Typical English |
|---|---|
| Perfective, dynamic verb, narrative | simple past — *he built* |
| Perfective, stative verb | simple present — *the price **is** not paid* |
| Perfective, stative verb + modal {ha} | *may he know*, *let it be* |
| Perfective, gnomic context | timeless present — *no man escapes* |
| Perfective + {u₃} | perfect participle — *having raised* |
| Perfective after *tukum-be₂* | *if he does not give* |
| Imperfective, present context | present — *he pours* |
| Imperfective, future context | *will build* |
| Imperfective, past narrative | past progressive — *was coming down* |
| Imperfective, legal document | *shall repay*, *is to be brought* |
| Imperfective of a stative verb | causative — *makes sweet*, *comes to know* |
| Imperfective non-finite ({ed} participle) | *to be given*, *in order to bring* |

## 8. What the writing hides

Three traps, all caused by the script rather than the language.

### Trap 1: a doubled sign is ambiguous

Sumerian uses **full** reduplication to mark plural events (many actions, many times, many
places) and **partial** reduplication to build imperfective stems. These are different
operations. The writing system spells both by writing the sign twice.

Jagersma's worked example:

> The Ur III messenger texts often contain the participle *dab₅-dab₅-de₃* 'in order to seize
> (runaways)'. That looks like evidence that *dab₅* has a reduplicated imperfective stem —
> until you notice the many *non*-reduplicated imperfectives of *dab₅* elsewhere, which prove
> the doubling here is a plural. (2010: §12.3)

"Unfortunately, such cross-checks are not always possible in the case of less frequently
occurring verbs." When a verb is rare, you may simply not be able to tell.

### Trap 2: scribes both under- and over-write

One and the same verbal form, in three Ur III letters:

```
(a) kishib-ba-ne₂ shu ha-ba-shi-ib₂-ti-ĝe₂₆     TCS 1:52 8  (Umma)
(b) kishib-ba-ne₂ shu ha-ba-shi-ib₂-ti          TCS 1:162 7 (Lagash)
(c) kishib-ba-ne₂ shu ha-ba-an-shi-ib₂-TI.TI    TCS 1:207 7 (Lagash)

'He should receive his sealed document from him.'
— Jagersma 2010: §12.3 ex. 13a–c
```

Only (a) approximates the real stem /teĝ/. (b) is abbreviated and gives no hint that a
special stem is involved at all. (c) writes a reduplication **that does not exist in the
spoken form**. Some scribes marked imperfectivity by doubling the sign regardless of what the
stem actually did (Krecher 1995: 165–169).

**Never read stem shape straight off the signs.** Read it off the agreement pattern and the
context.

### Trap 3: a stem can drift class over time

The verb *shesh* 'anoint' is treated as a reduplicating verb in texts after the third
millennium — but it certainly was not one earlier, where the imperfective participle is
plain *shesh₄-de₃*. Jagersma reconstructs the cause: in imperfective forms every possible
suffix begins with /e/, so */shesh+e/* was reanalysed as a stem */sheshe/*, and from there
as a reduplication (2010: §12.3). Aspect marking is not frozen; it drifts.

## 9. An unresolved technical dispute

If you read further in the literature you will run into an argument about a suffix that may
or may not exist. It is worth being able to recognise.

**Yoshikawa (1968)** proposed an *imperfective stem suffix* **{e}** — a marker attached to the
stem itself, which he also saw embedded in the {ed} morpheme (analysed as /e/ + /d/). On this
view the imperfective person suffixes *-en, -e, -enden, -enzen, -enē* all contain that same
*-e* plus a reduced personal element. This became known as the **marû -e**, and it implied
that the perfective/imperfective contrast is fundamentally about *stems*.

**Edzard** argued the opposite: the *-e* is simply part of the person suffixes used in the
imperfective conjugation, and Yoshikawa's "affixation class" is really just the class of verbs
whose stem does not change at all.

**Jagersma sides with Edzard** and gives the decisive objection: Yoshikawa's suffix is
"completely invisible outside the third person singular." The initial /e/ of the person
suffixes behaves identically in perfective and imperfective forms — same assimilations, same
reductions, same spellings — so positing an extra morpheme "has no basis in linguistic
reality" (2010: §15.3.1). He treats *-e* as a portmanteau\*: one morpheme doing two jobs,
marking both third-person-singular-transitive-subject and imperfectivity.

His conclusion on the wider question is a good one to carry with you:

> "The perfective and imperfective paradigms are formally distinct partly because of a
> different inflection and partly because of a difference in stem form. The former dominates
> the transitive paradigm and the latter the intransitive."
> — Jagersma 2010: §15.3.1

Neither mechanism is *the* answer. Both are.

## Notes on terminology

Terms marked with an asterisk\* in the text, in the order they appear.

**ḫamṭu** — Akkadian for 'quick'. The name Old Babylonian scribes gave to the Sumerian
**perfective** form, revived by Yoshikawa in 1968 and still standard in dictionaries. The
form given as a verb's citation form.

**marû** — Akkadian for 'slow, fat'. The scribes' name for the Sumerian **imperfective**.

**relative tense** — A tense system in which a form locates the event not against the moment
of speaking, but against some other reference point that the context supplies. Zólyomi
analyses Sumerian this way: the "preterite" marks anteriority to that reference point, the
"present-future" marks simultaneity or posteriority. It is what lets a "present-future" form
describe a past event.

**morphemic analysis / glossing** — Breaking a written word into its smallest meaningful
pieces (*morphemes*) and labelling each one. It is how Sumerologists show their working.

**portmanteau morpheme** — A single affix that carries two grammatical meanings at once,
which cannot be separated into parts. Jagersma treats the imperfective suffix *-e* as one: it
marks third person singular transitive subject *and* imperfective aspect simultaneously.

**stative** — Describing a state rather than an event: *know*, *be*, *have*, *be sweet*, as
opposed to *build*, *pour*, *run*. In Sumerian, stative meaning always requires the
perfective — the reverse of the pattern in Russian and most European aspect languages.

**split-ergative** — Said of a language that follows an ergative pattern in one part of its
grammar and a different pattern elsewhere. Sumerian's perfective agreement is ergative; its
imperfective agreement is partly accusative and partly tripartite.

**tripartite** — A pattern in which all three of A (transitive subject), S (intransitive
subject) and P (object) are marked differently, rather than any two being grouped together.

**verbal number / plural reduplication** — Repeating the *whole* stem to mark that the event
happened many times, in many places, or to many things. Different in function from the
*partial* reduplication that builds imperfective stems — but written the same way.

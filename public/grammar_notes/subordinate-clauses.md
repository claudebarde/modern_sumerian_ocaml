# Subordinate clauses

> **A note on spelling.** This page uses the transliteration conventions of the Modern Sumerian project:
> **š → sh**, **ḫ → h**, **ŋ → ĝ**. Examples quoted from the reference grammars have been
> re-spelled to match and normalised to the index-number style (_é_ → **e₂**, _ĝá_ → **ĝa₂**).
> Jagersma's special sign **ř** is normalised to its conventional readings (_řú_ → **du₃**,
> _ře₆_ → **de₆**).
>
> **Determinatives.** Some signs were written next to a word only to say what kind of thing it
> names, and were never pronounced. Following the ATF convention of ePSD2 and ORACC, these
> **determinatives**\* are written between curly braces: **{d}** before the name of a god
> (_{d}nanshe_), **{ki}** after the name of a place (_lagash{ki}_), **{ĝesh}** before the name
> of a wooden object (_{ĝesh}gu-za_ 'chair') and **{kush}** before a leather one
> (_{kush}a-ĝa₂-la₂_ 'bag'). They belong to the spelling on the tablet but not to the word
> itself, so the structural parse lines leave them out. Do not confuse them with the curly
> braces used for verbal morphemes in the running text, such as {u} or {'a}.

> **A note on periods.** Sumerian was written for some three thousand years, and its grammar
> changed. Following the _Electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary_ (ePSD2), every example on
> this page is labelled with the period of the tablet it comes from, and where a construction
> belongs to one period only, the text says so.
>
> | Abbreviation  | Period                  | Approximate dates | Typical sources                                                   |
> | ------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
> | **PC**        | Proto-cuneiform         | c. 3350–3000 BCE  | Uruk IV–III accounts                                              |
> | **ED IIIa**   | Early Dynastic IIIa     | c. 2600–2500 BCE  | Fara, Abu Salabikh                                                |
> | **ED IIIb**   | Early Dynastic IIIb     | c. 2500–2340 BCE  | Presargonic Lagash: Ur-Nanshe, E-ana-tum, En-metena, Uru-inimgina |
> | **Ebla**      | Ebla                    | c. 2350–2250 BCE  | the Ebla archives (Syria)                                         |
> | **OAkk**      | Old Akkadian (Sargonic) | c. 2340–2200 BCE  | Adab, Isin, Nippur; Sargonic royal inscriptions                   |
> | **Lag II**    | Lagash II               | c. 2200–2100 BCE  | Gudea, Nam-mahne                                                  |
> | **Ur III**    | Third Dynasty of Ur     | c. 2112–2004 BCE  | Umma, Drehem, Nippur, Lagash archives; royal inscriptions         |
> | **OB**        | Old Babylonian          | c. 2000–1600 BCE  | most literary manuscripts (ETCSL), school texts                   |
> | **Post-OB**   | after Old Babylonian    | after c. 1600 BCE | Middle Babylonian, Neo-Assyrian and later copies; bilinguals      |
> | **(unknown)** | —                       | —                 | tablets that cannot be dated                                      |
>
> Dates are approximate (middle chronology). Older grammars use broader labels that map onto
> these: **Old Sumerian** ≈ ED IIIb, **Sargonic** = OAkk, **Neo-Sumerian** ≈ Lag II + Ur III.
> Our main reference grammar (Jagersma 2010) is built on texts from ED IIIb to Ur III;
> constructions labelled **OB** come mostly from literary manuscripts copied after Sumerian had
> ceased to be spoken.

> **How to read the examples on this page.** Most examples have three lines. Line 1 is the
> transliteration as written on the tablet. Line 2 is a **structural parse**: square brackets
> mark where the subordinate clause begins and ends, `-'a` is the nominalising suffix, and the
> case marker that follows the bracket tells you what job the whole clause is doing. Line 3 is
> the translation. Sources follow, with the city and the period of the tablet
> (L = Lagash, U = Umma, N = Nippur, D = Drehem, I = Isin, A = Adab; periods as in the table
> above).

## 1. The problem: Sumerian has almost no conjunctions

An English speaker builds complex sentences out of small linking words:

> _The man **who** built the temple said **that**, **when** the king came, **because** the
> canal was dry, he would wait **until** it rained, **if** he could._

_Who, that, when, because, until, if_ — remove them and English falls apart. Sumerian has
exactly **three** subordinating conjunctions in the whole third-millennium corpus:

> "Most English subordinate clauses are introduced by conjunctions such as _when_, _that_, or
> _because_. This type of subordinate clause is quite rare in Sumerian. The texts of our corpus
> contain only the subordinating conjunctions _u₄-da_ 'if', _tukum-be₂_ 'if', and _en-na_
> 'until'."
> — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.2

So how does Sumerian say _when_, _because_, _that_, _who_?

### The master key

It turns the clause **into a noun**, and then gives that noun a **case ending**.

That is the whole trick, and once it clicks, most of this page becomes predictable. You have
already learned the case endings (see the _[noun cases](#)_ page). A subordinate clause simply
borrows them:

| English                | Sumerian                                     | Literally                  |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| _when_ he built it     | [he built it]**-a** + locative **-a**        | 'in his-having-built-it'   |
| _after_ he built it    | [he built it]**-a** + ablative **-ta**       | 'from his-having-built-it' |
| _until_ he builds it   | [he builds it]**-a** + terminative **-she₃** | 'towards his-building-it'  |
| _like_ he built it     | [he built it]**-a** + equative **-gen₇**     | 'like his-having-built-it' |
| _in order to_ build it | [build-**ed**] + directive **-e**            | 'to building it'           |

> "Most subordinate clauses are nominalized clauses. A nominalized clause […] can be used as a
> noun phrase in its own right, functioning as the subject or object of a clause, expressing a
> possessor, or having any other syntactic function which a noun phrase can have. Just like any
> other noun phrase, such a nominalized clause is in the case which is appropriate for the
> function it performs."
> — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.1

**If you remember one sentence from this page, make it this one: a Sumerian subordinate clause
is a noun phrase that happens to contain a verb.**

## 2. The map

Every Sumerian subordinate clause can be described along two axes.

**By form** — what the verb looks like (Jagersma 2010: §27.3.1):

1. **Nominalised clauses** — the overwhelming majority. Either a **finite** verb with the
   nominalising suffix **-a**, or a **non-finite** verb (a participle).
2. **Clauses with a conjunction** — _u₄-da_, _tukum-be₂_, _en-na_. Rare.
3. **Clauses with the prefix {u}** — a finite verb with the "relative-past" prefix **u₃-**,
   meaning 'having done X'. Common, and the only type that needs no suffix and no case.
   **By function** — what the clause does in the sentence (Jagersma 2010: §27.1):

- **Relative clauses** describe a noun: \*the man **who built the temple\***. → Part One
- **Complement clauses** are the subject or object of a verb or noun: \*he said **that she had
  run off\***. → Part Two
- **Adverbial clauses** say when, why, how or under what condition: **_when he came_**,
  **_because he was ill_**, **_if he does not pay_**. → Part Three
  Put the two axes together and you get the shape of this page:

|                | **Finite (-a)** | **Non-finite (participle)** | **Conjunction**          | **{u}-prefix** |
| -------------- | --------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------- |
| **Relative**   | §5–6            | §7                          | —                        | —              |
| **Complement** | §8–9            | §10                         | —                        | —              |
| **Time**       | §11             | §11                         | _en-na_ §11              | §12            |

## 3. The engine: the nominalising suffix -a

Zólyomi calls it the **subordinator**\* and puts it in the last slot of the verb, **S15**
(2017: §7.2). Jagersma calls it the **nominalising suffix**\* {'a} (2010: §27.3.3). Same thing.

> "Although it attaches to the verb as the last constituent of the clause, it affects in fact
> not the verb but the clause. It transforms the clause of the verb into a subordinate clause."
> — Zólyomi 2017: §7.2

It is always the **last suffix** of the verb — after the person suffix, before any clitic
(Jagersma 2010: §27.3.3):

```
main clause:       ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-a mu-ti-la            'the ruler stayed in the Emi'
subordinate:       ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-a mu-ti-la-a          'that the ruler stayed in the Emi'
                                        ^^ -a
+ locative:        ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-a mu-ti-la-a          'when the ruler stayed in the Emi'
                                          (-a + -a contract — see below)
```

### What -a looks like on the tablet

Its underlying form is /'a/, beginning with a glottal stop. After a **vowel** the glottal stop
survives and you see a plain **-a**. After a **consonant** the glottal stop assimilates, and the
scribe writes a CV-sign repeating that consonant (Jagersma 2010: §27.3.3):

| After                  | Written                 | Attested examples                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a vowel                | **-a**                  | _in-shi-sa₁₀-a_, _mu-ni-gi₄-a_, _i₃-me-a_, _ba-an-tuku-a_                                                              |
| b, d, g (plain stops)  | **-ba, -da, -ga**       | _in-dab₅-ba-a_, _in-pad₃-da_, _bi₂-in-du₁₁-ga_                                                                         |
| p, t (aspirated stops) | — never occurs          | no Sumerian verb (indeed no morpheme at all) ends in /p/ or /t/                                                        |
| k (aspirated stop)     | **-ka** or **-a**       | only two verbs end in /k/: _ak_ 'do' and _lu₅.k_ 'live' — _e-da-ak-ka-am₆_, _mu-da-lu₅-ka-am₆_ (but also _ba-ak-a-ta_) |
| z, ř (affricates)      | **-za, -ra**, or **-a** | _im-gaz-za-ta_, _i₃-kux-ra-a_, _e-ta-ku₅-ra_ (but also _nu-un-gaz-a_, _al-ku₅-a_)                                      |
| m, n, ĝ                | **-ma, -na, -ĝa₂**      | _e-na-shum₂-ma-a_, _mu-da-ĝen-na-a_, _an-na-aĝ₂-ĝa₂-ne₂_                                                               |
| l, r                   | **-la, -ra**            | _mu-ti-la-a_, _e-me-ĝar-ra-a_, _i₃-in-ku₄-ra-a_                                                                        |
| s                      | usually **-sa**         | _ab-us₂-sa_ (but also _ab-us₂-a_)                                                                                      |
| sh, h                  | usually **-a**          | _ba-ush₂-a_, _i₃-zah₃-a_ (but also _ba-ush₂-sha₄-ta_)                                                                  |
| glottal stop           | always **-a**           | _in-la₂-a_, _an-da-bala-a_                                                                                             |

**Why _p_ and _t_ never take the nominaliser.** Remember from the _[pronunciation](#)_ page that
Sumerian _b, d, g_ were plain voiceless [p, t, k], while _p, t, k_ were **aspirated**
[pʰ, tʰ, kʰ]. The aspirated stops were generally lost at the end of a syllable: "There is no
Sumerian morpheme with a /t/ or a /p/ in that position and only a few with a /k/"
(Jagersma 2010: §3.2.2, cf. §3.10). Since no verbal form can end in /p/ or /t/, the nominaliser
never has to follow them. Even /k/ is marginal — the only verbs with a final /k/ are _ak_ 'do,
make' and _lu₅.k_ 'live', and in both the /k/ tended to drop, which is why _-a_ alone also
appears after them.

**Practical tip.** When you see a verb ending in a doubled consonant + _a_ — _-ga_, _-ra_,
_-na_ — suspect a subordinate clause. That _-Ca_ is usually the nominaliser wearing the
previous consonant as a coat.

### Three spelling traps

**1. -a + locative -a contracts.** The most common subordinate clause in Sumerian is a
nominalised clause in the locative ('when …'). Three syllables /V'a'a/ shrink to two. After a
vowel you normally see a single _-a_; after a consonant, _-Ca-a_ (Jagersma 2010: §27.3.3):

```
u₄ e₂ {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ka mu-du₃-a             'when he built Ningirsu's temple'
                         ^^^^ single -a after a vowel      (Gudea Statue B 5:21–22; L; Lag II)

mu-na-diri-ga-a                            'when he made it rise above (the mountains) for her'
          ^^^^^ -ga-a after a consonant                    (En-ana-tum I 28 1:14; L; ED IIIb)
```

**2. Coordinated clauses share one -a.** When several subordinate clauses are strung together,
only the **last** verb needs the nominaliser (Wilcke 1990a: 464–470):

```
mu ba-gaz e₂ hulu-a i₃-me-a-she₃
[mu [ba-gaz] [e₂ hulu-a i₃-me-'a]=ak]=she
'because he had been killed and the estate was destroyed'
MVN 2:2 case 3 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.3 ex. 25
```

_ba-gaz_ has no -a. It still belongs inside the subordinate construction.

**3. The verb _ak_ 'do, make' usually drops the -a.** Nobody knows why, and its participles
behave the same way (Jagersma 2010: §27.3.6, §28.7):

```
mu ur-mes-e sa-gaz in-na-ak-she₃
[mu [ur-mes-e sa-gaz in-na-ak]=ak]=she      ← no -a
'because Urmes had robbed him'
NG 42 5 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.6 ex. 38
```

### A fossil: subordinate clauses with no -a at all

A tiny handful survive. The most useful is a frozen circumstantial construction with the verb
_zu_ 'know', meaning '**without X knowing it**' (Jagersma 2010: §27.3.5):

```
ur-{d}lama₃ dam-e nu-u₃-zu-be₂     lu₂-kur₂ in-da-nu₂-a
[ur-lama dam-e nu-i-n-zu]=be=e
'that a stranger had slept with her without Ur-Lama, (her) husband, knowing it'
NG 205 21–22 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.5 ex. 32
```

And one relative clause with no -a, in ED IIIb Lagash:

```
an-ta-sur-ra e₂ me-lam₂-be₂ kur-kur-ra a-dul₅
'the Antasurra, the temple whose radiance covers the mountain lands'
En-metena 8 6:1–2 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.5 ex. 33
```

## 4. The other engine: four participles

Sumerian can also subordinate with a **non-finite** verb — a participle. A participle has no
person prefixes, no dimensional prefixes, no modal prefixes. It may carry only the negative
_nu-_ (Jagersma 2010: §28.1).

| Form               | Jagersma's name              | Zólyomi's name      | Built as                  | Typical meaning                              |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **ĝar**            | present participle           | tenseless (TL)      | bare stem                 | generic, timeless: _one who places_          |
| **ĝar-ra**         | past participle              | preterite (PT)      | stem **-a**               | specific, usually past: _placed, who placed_ |
| **ĝa₂-ĝa₂-de₃**    | imperfective participle      | present-future (PF) | imperfective stem **-ed** | future, obligation, purpose: _to be placed_  |
| **ĝa₂-ĝa₂-da-ne₂** | imperfective participle + -a | —                   | **-ed-a**                 | only in the "pronominal conjugation" (§11)   |

(Jagersma 2010: §28.1; Zólyomi 2017: §7.1)

Two properties make Sumerian participles very unlike English ones, and both matter when you read.

**They are both nouns and adjectives.** English needs a gerund (_building takes time_) and a
participle (_the building man_). Sumerian uses one form for both. _du₃-a_ can be 'built' or
'(the) building' (Jagersma 2010: §28.1).

**They are unoriented\*.** An English past participle always points at the thing done-to: _the
hated enemy_ = the enemy is hated. A Sumerian participle can point at **any** participant:

> "The Sumerian past participle _du₃-a_ can have the same meaning as the English and German past
> participles _built_ and _gebaut_, but it can also have the meanings 'who built', 'for whom was
> built', and so on."
> — Jagersma 2010: §28.1

And they are neither active nor passive: _shum₂-ma_ can mean 'who gave' or 'which was given'.
Only context decides.

### Present participle: generic and timeless

```
lugal gabari nu-tuku          'a king who has no rival'
                              (with nu-tuku-a it would mean 'a king who, at some point, has
                               not had a rival') — Zólyomi 2017: §7.1

6 sila₄ nu-ur₄                'six unshorn lambs'  (a permanent property)
                              OIP 14:172 5 (A; OAkk)
66+[x] sila₄ nu-ur₄-ra i₃-ĝal₂ 'there were 66+x lambs which were not shorn'  (a specific event)
                              Bridges Mesag NBC 6983 (L; OAkk) — Jagersma 2010: §28.2.1 exx. 6–7
```

That pair is the cleanest illustration of the difference: _nu-ur₄_ describes what kind of lamb
it is; _nu-ur₄-ra_ reports what happened at shearing time.

Present participles are the source of most Sumerian **job titles**: _dub-sar_ 'scribe' = 'one
who writes tablets', _kug-dim₂_ 'goldsmith' = 'one who fashions metal' (Zólyomi 2017: §7.1).
They are headless relative clauses frozen into words.

# PART ONE — Relative clauses

## 5. Finite relative clauses: the basic pattern

A relative clause describes a noun. In Sumerian:

> **head noun → relative clause → then the head noun's own case marker**

```
alan lu₂ e₂ {d}ba-u₂ mu-du₃-a-kam
alan [lu₂ [e₂ bau=ak mu-n-du-'a]]=ak=am
'This is the statue of the man who built the temple of Bau.'
Gudea Statue E 9:6–8 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 40
```

Look where the genitive _-ak_ of _lu₂_ 'man' ended up: on the end of the relative clause,
fused into _-kam_. **The case marker of the head noun follows the whole relative clause.** That
is the single most important thing to know about reading relative clauses — exactly as the
_[noun cases](#)_ page showed for any noun phrase.

### There is no relative pronoun

English needs _who / whom / which / whose / where_. Sumerian has none.

> "There are no relative pronouns. As a rule, the same person markers that always express a
> given grammatical function in a clause are also used to indicate the function of the head
> noun in the relative clause."
> — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1

So you find the head noun's role **inside the verb**. If the verb has a 3rd person A prefix
_-n-_ referring back to the head, the head is the subject; if it has an ablative _-ta-_, the
head is 'from which'; and so on. Zólyomi: "the syntactic function of the head noun within the
relative clause is marked in Sumerian not with a relative pronoun but with a pronominal affix of
the finite verb" (2017: §7.3).

### Any role can be relativised

| Role of the head                 | Example                                                                                                                                      | Source                             |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **transitive subject** 'who'     | _lu₂ e₂ {d}nanna in-du₃-a_ 'the man who built the temple of Nanna'                                                                           | Ur-Namma 1 3–4 (Ur; Ur III)        |
|                                  | _ensi₂ inim bi₂-ib₂-gi₄-gi₄-a_ 'the ruler who revokes the command'                                                                           | Gudea St. B 1:13–14 (L; Lag II)    |

| **intransitive subject** 'which' | _ĝesh tir abbar{ki}-ka i₃-gub-ba_ 'trees which stood in the forest of Abbar'                                                                 | VS 14:178 3:2–3 (L; ED IIIb)       |
|                                  | _mu ab-us₂-sa_ 'the year that follows it'                                                                                                    | NATN 109 15 (N; Ur III)            |
|                                  | _u₄ ki-shar₂-ra ma-ra-ta-e₃-a_ 'the light that came forth for you on the horizon'                                                            | Gudea Cyl. A 5:19 (L; Lag II)      |
| **direct object** 'which'        | _udu gab₂-us₂-e ba-an-la-ha_ 'the sheep which the assistant shepherd drove off'                                                              | NG 138 14 (U; Ur III)              |

| **source** 'from which'          | _gur₄-gur₄ kug luh-ha i₃ iti-da {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄ ab-ta-gu₇-a_ 'the vessel of purified silver from which Ningirsu consumes the monthly oil' | En-metena 7 15–16 (L; ED IIIb)     |

| **place** 'where'                | _ki ME-silim-e na bi₂-du₃-a_ 'the place where Mesilim had set up stelas'                                                                     | E-ana-tum 6 4:16–17 (L; ED IIIb)   |
| **possessor** 'whose'            | _lu₂ 3 shuku-be₂ i₃-la₂-a_ 'the three men whose prebends fall short'                                                                         | NG 215 5 (U; Ur III)               |
|                                  | _zi-da gabu₂-na piriĝ i₃-nu₂-nu₂-a_ '(she) on whose right and left lions were lying'                                                         | Gudea Cyl. A 5:16 (L; Lag II)      |

(All Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 exx. 41–54; Zólyomi 2017: §7.3 exx. 107–110, 120)

Zólyomi's summary: "There seems to be no restriction on the syntactic function of the
participants to be relativized" (2017: §7.3).

**Whose** works by planting a possessive pronoun inside the relative clause that points back to
the head: _lu₂ 3 **shuku-be₂** i₃-la₂-a_ — literally 'the three men **their-prebend** is short'.

### A possessive after the relative clause belongs to the head

Because phrase-final clitics come at the very end, a possessive pronoun after the relative clause
belongs to the **head**, not the verb (Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1):

```
{ĝesh}dur₂-ĝar lu₂ mu-na-de₆-a-ne₂
[dur₂ĝar [lu₂-e mu-nna-n-de-'a]]=ane
'his throne which one had brought to him'
Gudea Statue B 9:10 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 68
```

```
shul zi lu₂ igi mu-bar-ra-zu
[shul zid lu₂ [igi mu-e-bar-'a]]=zu=ra
'for the righteous young man at whom you look'   (lit. 'your … man')
Gudea Cyl. A 3:5 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 64
```

### 'Whoever', 'whatever', 'as much as'

The interrogatives **a-na** 'what?' and **a-ba** 'who?' inside a relative clause mean
**whatever** and **whoever** (Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1, §8.5):

```
a₂-aĝ₂-ĝa₂ a-shag₄-ga a-na bi₂-du₁₁-ga
'the orders about the field, whichever I gave'
TCS 1:109 14–15 (Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 55
```

And _en-na_ — normally 'until' — means '**as much as, as many as**' in a relative clause
(lit. 'up to what …'):

```
she-numun ur-{d}kush₇-{d}ba-u₂-ke₄ en-na gu₃ ba-de₂-a
'seed barley, as much as Ur-Kush-Bau calls for'
AuOr 17–18 p. 218 1 4–5 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 56
```

### Restrictive and non-restrictive

Most Sumerian relative clauses are **restrictive**\* — they pick out _which_ one. But
non-restrictive ones exist too, most obviously after proper names, which cannot be restricted
(Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1, following Huber 2000: 102–109):

```
{d}suen-bur-shu₄ gukkal-she₃ im-shi-ĝen-na
'Suen-burshu, who came for fat-tailed sheep'
RTC 378 4–5 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 58
```

And there is a special **appositive** construction that is always non-restrictive: a general noun
like **lu₂** 'man', **ki** 'place' or **e₂** 'house' as head, placed in apposition:

```
diĝir gu₃-de₂-a ensi₂ lagash{ki} lu₂ e₂-an-na in-du₃-a-kam
'He is the god of Gudea, the ruler of Lagash, (the man) who built the Eanna.'
Gudea Statue C 1:2–6 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.1 ex. 60
```

This _lu₂ … -a_ construction is the closest Sumerian comes to an English _who_, and it is very
common in royal inscriptions.

## 6. Headless relative clauses: 'the one who', 'what'

Drop the head noun and the relative clause becomes a noun phrase in its own right, taking its
**own** case marker (Jagersma 2010: §27.4.2):

```
e₂-ĝu₁₀ ma-du₃-na
[e₂=ĝu ma-du-en-'a]
'you, who will build my temple for me'
Gudea Cyl. A 9:8 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.2 ex. 70

{d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄ iri-ka-ge-na-da e-da-du₁₁-ga-a  shu nu-di-ni-bala-e
[ninĝirsuk-e irikagenak-da i-n-da-n-dug-'a]='a
'What Ningirsu has agreed with Irikagena he will not change.'
Uru-inimgina 34 1 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.4 ex. 30

e₂ du₆ me-kul-ab₄-ta-ka-ta i₃-kux-a-am₆
'These (beams) are what went in from the House of Mekulabta's Hill.'
DP 438 2:2–3 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.4.2 ex. 72
```

Several ordinary Sumerian **nouns** are frozen headless relative clauses (Jagersma 2010:
§27.3.5, §6.6.2):

- **ba-ush₂** 'dead person' = _ba-ush₂_ 'one who has died'
- **ma-an-sim** 'sieve' = 'one in which it is sieved for me'

## 7. Non-finite relative clauses (participles)

The same jobs can be done with a participle instead of a finite verb. Zólyomi shows that the two
are genuinely interchangeable — the **same idiom**, finite in one line and non-finite a few
columns later (2017: §7.3 exx. 112–113):

```
FINITE:      inim {d}nanshe-e mu-na-du₁₁-ga-ash  saĝ sig ba-shi-ĝar
             'He accepted the words that Nanshe told him.'        Gudea Cyl. A 7:11–12

NON-FINITE:  inim du₁₁-ga {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ka-she₃  saĝ sig ba-shi-ĝar
             'He accepted the words told by Ningirsu.'            Gudea Cyl. A 12:14–15
```

### With a past participle (-a)

```
5 gu₄ ur-re dab₅-ba            'five bulls caught by lions'        Nik 2:440 1 (U; Ur III)
5 ur-gi₇ e₂-gal-ta er-ra       'five dogs which went out of the palace'  Nik 2:440 1 (U; Ur III)
dub-sar lugal a-shag₄ gid₂-da-a 'the royal scribes who surveyed the fields' (erg.)  TROM 2:407 4–5 (U; Ur III)
ĝesh-ge izi gu₇-a              'reed consumed by fire'             TENS 465 2 (U; Ur III)
e₂ me-lam₂-be₂ an-ne₂ us₂-sa   'the house whose awesomeness touches heaven'  Gudea Cyl. A 17:18 (L; Lag II)
ki-ĝal₂ ĝesh nu-gub-ba-am₆     'This is fallow land where no trees stand.'  DP 610 2:2 (L; ED IIIb)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §28.3.3 exx. 70–88)

### The _Mesanepada_ construction: how to say 'by whom'

A participle has no person prefix, so where does the **agent** go? Two places
(Zólyomi 2017: §7.3; Jagersma 2010: §28.3.3):

**(a) Before the participle, in the ergative** — called the _Mesanepada construction_ after the
royal name _mesh₃-an-ne₂-pad₃-da_ 'young man chosen **by An**' (_an=e pad-'a_):

```
dumu an kug-ge tud-da
[dumu [an kug=e tud-'a]]
'the child born by the holy god An'
Gudea Cyl. A 2:28 (L; Lag II) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 117
```

**(b) After the participle, in the genitive** — and this is the commoner way when the head is
missing or the phrase is a royal epithet:

```
dumu tud-da {d}ĝa₂-tum₃-dug₁₀-ke₄
[dumu tud-'a ĝatumdug=ak]=e
'the child born by the goddess Gatumdug'   (lit. 'the born child of Gatumdug')
Gudea Statue B 2:16–17 (L; Lag II) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 116
```

Royal inscriptions are built almost entirely out of (b). Read the opening of Lugal-zagesi's
inscription with this in mind and it becomes a list of headless past participles with genitive
agents: _ĝeshtug₂ shum₂-ma {d}en-ki_ 'given wisdom by Enki', _mu pad₃-da {d}utu_ 'called by name by
Utu', _dumu tud-da {d}nisaba_ 'child born by Nisaba', _ga zid gu₇-a {d}nin-hur-saĝ_ 'fed true milk
by Ninhursag' (Lugal-zagesi 1 1:3–35, quoted as an exercise in Zólyomi 2017: Lesson 7).

### With a present participle (bare stem)

Generic, permanent properties:

```
il ensi₂ umma{ki}-a a-shag₄ kar-kar  niĝ₂-erim₂ du₁₁-du₁₁-ge
'Il, the ruler of Umma, who takes away fields, who says vicious things'
En-metena 28 4:19–23 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §28.2.2 ex. 10

{ĝesh}shar₂-ur₃ me₃-a kur shu-she₃ ĝar-ĝar
'the Sharur weapon, which subjugates the foreign lands in battle'
Gudea Cyl. B 7:19 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §28.2.2 ex. 11

kur gu₂ ĝar-ĝar {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ka-ke₄
'he who makes all the foreign lands submit for Ningirsu'
E-ana-tum 4 1:9–10 (L; ED IIIb) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 118
```

### With an imperfective participle (-ed): 'to be', 'can', 'cannot'

```
she ur₅-ra su-su               'a barley loan which is to be repaid'      ITT 3:5938 3 (L; Ur III)
kur {ĝesh}erin-na lu₂ nu-ku₄-ku₄-da
                               'into the cedar mountains, where no one can enter'
                                                                          Gudea Cyl. A 15:19 (L; Lag II)
{d}suen mu-ne₂ lu₂ nu-duh-de₃    'Suen, whose name no one can "release"'    Gudea St. B 8:48 (L; Lag II)
lugal a₂ dugud-da-ne₂ kur-e nu-il₂-e
                               'the king whose heavy arm no foreign land can bear'
                                                                          Gudea St. D 5:2–3 (L; Lag II)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §28.4.1, §28.4.3 exx. 162, 191, 200, 202)

A negated imperfective participle = **impossibility**. That _nu- … -ed_ pattern is the Sumerian
'cannot'.

### Headless participles that became nouns

```
du₁₁-ga  'statement'  (= 'what was said')      zi-ga   'levy'      (= 'what was raised')
la₂-a    'deficit'    (= 'what is short')       sar-ra  'document'  (= 'what was written')
kas₄     'runner'     (= 'one who runs')        dub-sar 'scribe'    (= 'one who writes tablets')
```

(Jagersma 2010: §28.2.2, §28.3.3; Zólyomi 2017: §7.1)

# PART TWO — Complement clauses ('that …')

A **complement clause**\* is a clause that serves as the subject or object of a verb or noun:
\*he said **that she had run off\***. Sumerian builds them, like everything else, from a
nominalised clause — and gives it the case its job requires (Jagersma 2010: §27.5).

## 8. With verbs: _zu_ 'know', _ge-en_ 'confirm', _dug₄_ 'say'

Only a handful of verbs take finite complement clauses in our corpus, and with them the clause is
the **direct object**, in the **absolutive**:

```
ba-ba nagar-e igi di-ku₅-ne-she₃ mu lugal in-pad₃-da  ka-ga-na ba-ni-ge-en₆
                                 [mu lugal=ak in-pad-'a]=ø
'That Baba, the carpenter, had taken an oath by the king's name before the judges, he himself
 confirmed with his own mouth.'
NG 131 20–23 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2 ex. 78

i₃-zah₃-a in-na-an-du₁₁
[i-zah-'a]=ø
'He said to him that she had run off.'
SNAT 519 4 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2 ex. 80

ki-aĝ₂ … pesh-tur-tur dumu ur-{d}suen urdu₂-ĝu₁₀ in-sa₁₀-a bi₂-in-du₁₁
'Kiag said that Urdugu had bought Peshturtur, the daughter of Ur-Suen.'
ZA 55 p. 79 1–4 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2 ex. 79

mu sar-ra-be₂ ab-ta-UL₄-a  ĝeshtug₂-ne₂ al-zu-zu-a
'he whose ear will learn that this inscription was effaced from it'
E-ana-tum 62 Face A 4:4'–7' (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2 ex. 77
```

### Direct speech is the norm

With verbs of speaking, Sumerian **prefers direct speech**. The quotation simply sits where an
object would (Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2):

```
ma-an-shum₂-na-a geme₂-ĝu₁₀ nu-ra-shum₂ bi₂-in-du₁₁
'Manshumna said: "I did not give my slave woman to you".'
NG 195 6 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2 ex. 84

u₄-da she nu-tuku ib₂-be₂
'if he says: "I have no barley."'
TCS 1:157 7 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.2 ex. 85
```

Recall the rule from the _[Perfective and imperfective](#)_ page: a verb of speaking **after**
the quotation is perfective (_bi₂-in-du₁₁_); one **before** it is imperfective
(_mu-na-ni-ib₂-gi₄-gi₄_, Gudea Cyl. A 5:11).

### Turning direct into indirect speech

Jagersma has a minimal pair (2010: §27.5.2 exx. 88a–b):

```
DIRECT:    2 ma-na kug-babbar-am₃ ga-la₂ i₃-na-du₁₁
           '"It is two pounds of silver that I will pay," he said to him.'
           NATN 571 12–13 (N; Ur III)

INDIRECT:  10 giĝ₄ kug-babbar lugal-he₂-ĝal₂ i₃-la₂-e-a bi₂-in-du₁₁
           'Lugalhegal said that he would pay ten shekels of silver.'
           AAS 78 1–6 (U; Ur III)
```

Three things change: the main clause becomes a nominalised clause (_-a_); first person becomes
third; the modal _ga-_ becomes a plain declarative. **One thing does not change: the tense.**
English back-shifts _will pay_ → _would pay_. Sumerian does not.

## 9. With nouns: the oath, the witness, the document

Certain nouns refer to a speech event, and their content is a complement clause in the
**genitive** (Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3). The legal tablets are full of them:

| Noun                               | Meaning                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **mu lugal** (+ _pad₃_)            | '(oath by) the king's name' — 'he swore that …'       |
| **lu₂ inim-ma**                    | 'witness' (lit. 'man of the word') — 'witness that …' |
| **nam-erim₂** (+ _ku₅_)            | 'oath' — 'he swore an oath that …'                    |
| **dub**                            | 'tablet, document' — 'the document that …'            |
| **inim**                           | 'word, matter' — 'agreement that …'                   |
| **a₂-aĝ₂-ĝa₂**, **niĝ₂-mul-an-na** | 'message, news' — 'the news that …'                   |
| **en₃ … tar**                      | 'ask, investigate' — 'investigate whether …'          |

```
mu lugal nu-ub-kur₂-a in-pad₃
[mu lugal=ak [nu-ib-kur-e-'a]=ak]
'He took an oath by the king's name that he would not change it.'
AUCT 1:580 6 (Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3 ex. 89

lu₂ inim-ma {ĝesh}kiri₆ ur-{d}nanshe ba-na-gid₂-da-me
'They are the witnesses that the orchard was plotted for Ur-Nanshe.'
NG 11 13–14 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3 ex. 90; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 105

mash dam-gar₃ ba-a-ĝar-ra  en₃-be₂ tar-re-dam
'It is to be asked whether the merchant's interest was placed on it.'
MVN 11:162 8–9 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3 ex. 99
```

### The anticipatory genitive

Very often the clause is **pulled to the front**, and the noun carries a possessive _-be₂_ 'its'
pointing back to it — the same _left-dislocated genitive_ you met on the _[noun cases](#)_
page (Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3; Zólyomi 2017: §4.2):

```
hu-ru ama-ar-gi₄-me in-ĝar-ra   lu₂ inim-ma-be₂
'a witness that Huru established our freedom'
   (lit. 'of that Huru established our freedom — its witness')
NG 169 10–11 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3 ex. 93

tukum-be₂ … {ĝesh}kiri₆ du-du in-shi-sa₁₀-a  dub-be₂ nu-mu-de₆
'if I do not bring the document about that I bought Dudu's orchard from him'
NG 109 8–11 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3 ex. 94

nu-siki nu-ma-kush lu₂ a₂ tuku nu-na-ĝa₂-ĝa₂-a  {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-da iri-ka-ge-na-ke₄
inim-be₂ ka e-da-keshe₂
'Iri-kagina made an agreement with Ningirsu that he would not surrender the orphan or the widow
 to the powerful.'
Uru-inimgina 4 12:23–28 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.3 ex. 98; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 104
```

That last one — the most famous sentence of the Uru-inimgina reforms — is an anticipatory
complement clause. Once you can see the structure, you can read it.

## 10. Non-finite complements and other strategies

Complement clauses can also be **non-finite**, mostly with nouns:

```
lu₂ inim-ma inim til-a-kam            'He is the witness that the matter was completed.'
                                      BIN 8:172 16 (I; OAkk)
a₂-aĝ₂-ĝa₂ ugnim silim-ma             'the message that the army was safe'
                                      ITT 3:5271 14 (L; Ur III)
niĝ₂-mul-an-na lagash{ki} hulu-a          'the message that Lagash had been defeated'
                                      MVN 10:124 rev 10–11 (OAkk)
e₂-ĝu₁₀ du₃-da ĝiskim-be₂ ga-ra-ab-shum₂
                                      'I will give you the sign for building my temple!'
                                      Gudea Cyl. A 9:9 (L; Lag II)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §28.3.4, §28.4.4; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 101)

Sumerian also has several **complementation strategies**\* — other constructions that do the
same job (Jagersma 2010: §27.5.1, following Deutscher 2000):

**With _zu_ and _dug₄_, a non-finite complement goes in the locative**, 'about …':

```
e₂-a-ne₂ du₃-da ma-an-du₁₁
[e₂=ane du-ed]='a
'He spoke to me about building his temple.'  (Jagersma)
'He ordered me to build his temple.'           (Zólyomi)
Gudea Cyl. A 4:20 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §28.4.4 ex. 216; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 102
```

Note the two translations. The construction is the same; the grammarians disagree on how much
force _dug₄_ carries here (see §19).

**With _ge-en_ 'confirm, prove', a terminative 'as to …':**

```
a-tu ma₂ shum₂-ma-ash ba-ge-en₆
'Atu was proven to have given the boat.'   (lit. 'was made firm as one who gave the boat')
NG 62 11 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §28.3.3 ex. 111

saĝ ki min-a sa₁₀-ash ba-ge-en₆
'The slave was proven to have been sold twice.'
NG 69 11 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §28.3.3 ex. 112
```

**A purpose clause instead of a complement** — the two are "all but synonymous"
(Jagersma 2010: §28.4.4 exx. 233–234):

```
iti sheg₁₂-ga-ka gi₄-gi₄-da mu lugal-be₂ in-pad₃   (genitive complement)
iti sheg₁₂-ga-ka gi₄-gi₄-de₃ mu lugal-be₂ i₃-pad₃   (purpose, directive)
'He took an oath by the king's name to return it in month III.'
NRVN 1:104 6–8 (N; Ur III)
```

**Parataxis** — two main clauses side by side, no subordination at all:

```
e₂-gal-she₃ shu im-mi-nu-us₂  lugal-ĝu₁₀ ĝeshtug₂-ga-ne₂ he₂-zu
'He sent it to the palace. May my king hear it!'
   (= 'I sent it to the palace so that my king may know')
FAOS 19 Gir 12 rev. (L; OAkk) — Jagersma 2010: §27.5.1 ex. 75
```

# PART THREE — Adverbial clauses

Here the case marker does all the work. Each case gives a meaning (Jagersma 2010: §27.6.1):

| Case on the nominalised clause             | Gives                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| **locative** -a                            | _when, while_                           |
| **ablative** -ta                           | _after, since_                          |
| **terminative** -she₃                      | _until_; with a negative, _before_      |
| **genitive + terminative** after _mu_      | _because_                               |
| **genitive + adverbiative** -a-ke₄-esh     | _because_                               |
| **comitative** -da                         | _because_; _as long as_                 |
| **equative** -gen₇                         | _like, just as_; also _when_, _because_ |
| **directive** -e (imperfective participle) | _in order to_                           |

## 11. Time

### 'When' — three generations of the same construction

**(a) The oldest: a bare clause in the locative.** The norm in ED IIIb administrative texts
(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2):

```
sa₆-sa₆ iri kug-ga i₃-kux-ra-a   ĝesh be₂-tag
[sasag iri kug=a i-ni-kur-'a]='a
'When Sasag entered the Holy City, she sacrificed this (lamb).'
VS 14:5 1:3–5 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 102

ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-she₃ e-ĝen-na-a   e₂-muhaldim-ma ba-sa₆
'When the ruler came to the women's quarters, it (= a ram) was prepared in the kitchen.'
DP 218 1:7–2:1 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 104
```

**(b) A time-word as head: _u₄_ 'day', _mu_ 'year', _iti_ 'month'.** Elevated style at first —
royal inscriptions — then everywhere:

```
mu lugal akshak{ki}-ka i₃-zi-ga-a          'in the year when the king of Akshak rose up'
                                        E-ana-tum 2 4:25–26 (L; ED IIIb)
iti {d}nin-dar e₂ gibil-na i₃-kux-ra-a   'in the month when Nindar entered his new temple'
                                        VS 14:78 4:4 (L; ED IIIb)
u₄ na-wa-ar{ki}-ta i₃-im-e-re-esh-sha-a    'on the day when they came from Nawar'
                                        TSDU 38 9–10 (D; Ur III)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 exx. 107–109)

**(c) By the Ur III period, _u₄ … -a_ has won.**

> "In the Old Sumerian texts from Lagash, clauses with _u₄.d_ as their head noun are the most
> common type of temporal clause in the royal inscriptions, while in the administrative texts
> content clauses in the locative case are the norm. […] A few centuries later, in the Ur III
> period, clauses with _u₄.d_ as their head noun have become the norm in administrative texts,
> too."
> — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2

```
u₄ nam-gala-she₃ i₃-in-ku₄-ra-a    'when he entered the position of lamentation priest'
                                    MVN 8:122 10 (D; Ur III)
u₄ ur-kish{ki}-ta i₃-im-ĝen-na-a        'when he came from Urkish'
                                    TCL 2:5565 3–4 (D; Ur III)
```

Several coordinated _when_-clauses each keep their own locative, which for Jagersma proves they
are in apposition to _u₄_ (Zólyomi analyses the construction differently — see §19):

```
u₄ {d}nanshe nam-lugal lagash{ki}-sa mu-na-shum₂-ma-a  {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄ mu e-ne₂-pad₃-da-a
'on the day when Nanshe had given him the kingship over Lagash and Ningirsu had nominated him'
En-metena 26 13–18 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 110
```

**Future 'when' uses the imperfective** — even when the event is _anterior_ to the main clause:

```
u₄ geme₂-{d}lama₃ ba-ug₇-e-da-a … in-ba-a-ne
'When Geme-Lama dies, they will share him (= a slave).'
NG 7 15–19 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 115

u₄ temen-ĝu₁₀ ma-si-gi₄-na
'when you drive in my foundation pegs for me'
Gudea Cyl. A 11:18 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 114
```

**Non-finite 'when'** — a past participle in the locative:

```
ur-{d}nin-ĝesh-zi-da ti-la-a        'when Ur-Ningishzida was still alive'   NG 18 5 (L; Ur III)
iti 12 zal-la-a                    'when the twelfth month had passed'     VS 14:1 2:4 (L; ED IIIb)
35 mu zal-la-ba                    'when thirty-five years had passed'     Gutium 3 6–7 (OAkk)
iti ezem {d}lugal-urub{ki}-ka til-la-ba  'in the month when the Festival of Lugalurub ended'
                                                                           DP 85 2:4 (L; ED IIIb)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §28.3.4 exx. 131–135)

### 'After', 'since' — the ablative -ta

Three constructions (Jagersma 2010: §27.6.3):

```
(a) BARE CLAUSE IN THE ABLATIVE
a-ga-de₃{ki} nam-lugal shu ba-ab-ti-a-ta
'after Akkad had received the kingship'
DC 2 p. 57 4':9'–11' (L; OAkk)

ba-gara₂ e₂ id₂-de₃ la₂-a-e im-ma-ti-a-ta   ninda ĝesh bi₂-tag
'After he had reached the Bagara, the temple hanging over the river, he sacrificed bread.'
Gudea Cyl. A 2:7 (L; Lag II)

i₃-na-shum₂-ma-ta   mash i₃-na-a-ĝa₂-ĝa₂
'From when he gave it to him, interest will be placed on it for him.'
NRVN 1:2 12–13 (N; Ur III)

(b) WITH u₄ AS HEAD
u₄ lugal-ĝu₁₀ ba-ĝen-na-ta                 'after my king went away'
                                            BPOA 1:563 8 (U; Ur III)
u₄ e₂-gal-e ba-ab-tum₂-ma-ta  igi nu-ni-du₈-a
'that he had not seen him since the palace had taken him away'
NG 190 23–24 (L; Ur III)

(c) WITH eĝer 'back': 'from the back of …'
eĝer₄ in-taka₄-a-ta                         'after he had divorced her'
                                            NG 23 5 (L; Ur III)
eĝer₄ kesh₃{ki}-ta ba-ush₂-sha₄-ta               'after Keshta had died'
                                            DP 482 6:1–6 (L; ED IIIb)
eĝer₄ udu ur₄-ra-ta                         'after the sheep had been shorn'  (non-finite)
                                            VS 14:111 1:4 (L; ED IIIb)
```

Paired with the terminative, the ablative gives a span — _iti nesaĝ-ta / iti {d}dumu-zi-she₃_ 'from
the month Nesag to the month Dumuzi' (UTAMI 3:1692 4–5; U; Ur III).

### 'Until' — en-na and the terminative

**en-na** is one of the three genuine conjunctions. It usually comes first, though not always,
and is usually followed by an imperfective and a modal main clause (Jagersma 2010: §27.6.4):

```
en-na ensi₂-ra i₃-na-ab-be₂   kishib-ba-ne₂ he₂-ma-an-tum₂
'Until he speaks to the governor, he should bring me his sealed document!'
TCS 1:112 6–9 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.4 ex. 131
```

It also appears **together with** a terminative clause — 'until' marked twice:

```
ensi₂ en-na i₃-ĝen-na-ash   2-a-be₂ en-nu-ĝa₂ he₂-ti
[ensik en-na i-m-ĝen-'a]=she
'Until the governor comes, the two of them should remain in prison.'
TJAMC IOS 40 4–5 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.4 ex. 132
```

And a non-finite version: _u₄ 30 zal-la-ash_ 'until the thirtieth day has passed'
(CTMMA I 22 case 5; D; Ur III).

### 'Before' — a negative in the terminative

This one is easy to misread. A **negative** clause in the terminative means **before**:
literally 'until (it was) not X'. It is the exact functional parallel of Akkadian _adi lā_
(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.4):

```
6 {ĝesh}hashhur gur … kab₂ nu-ub-da-ab-du₁₁-ga-ash  … ur-{d}shara₂ dam-gar₃ in-na-shum₂
                     [nu-ib-da-b-dug-'a]=she
'Six gur of apples, worth five shekels of silver: Dalu and Ur-Ab'u gave this to Ur-Shara, the
 merchant, before one could estimate it.'
BM 105356 7–11 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.4 ex. 135
```

### 'While', 'as' — the pronominal conjugation

An archaic non-finite construction: a participle + a **possessive pronoun** that names the
subject + a case marker. Traditionally (and misleadingly) called the **pronominal
conjugation**\* (Jagersma 2010: §28.6; Zólyomi 2017: §7.5):

|             | Perfective (anterior)         | Imperfective (simultaneous)      |
| ----------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| 1 sg.       | _zi-ga-ĝu₁₀-ne_ 'when I rose' | _zi-zi-da-ĝu₁₀-ne_ 'when I rise' |
| 2 sg.       | _zi-ga-zu-ne_                 | _zi-zi-da-zu-ne_                 |
| 3 sg. human | _zi-ga-ne₂_                   | _zi-zi-da-ne₂_                   |
| 3 non-human | _zi-ga-be₂_                   | _zi-zi-da-be₂_                   |
| 3 pl.       | _zi-ga-ne-ne_                 | _zi-zi-da-ne-ne_                 |

(Jagersma 2010: §28.6; 1st and 2nd plural unattested)

```
ka₅-a ti-la-ne₂   shu ba-ti         'While Ka'a was alive, he received it.'
                                     BIN 8:352 2:2–4 (L; ED IIIb)
ur-saĝ e₂-a-na ku₄-ku₄-da-ne₂       'as the warrior was entering his house'
                                     Gudea Cyl. B 5:4 (L; Lag II)
she-be₂ ki-su₇-ka ĝal₂-la-be₂       'while this barley was on the threshing floor'
                                     NG 214 23 (U; Ur III)
lugal urim₅{ki}-she₃ du-ne₂             'when the king was going to Ur'
                                     SACT 1:109 7:5 (D; Ur III)
ĝe₂₆-e ĝen-na-ĝu₁₀-ne               'when I come'   TCTI 1:1036 3:4 (L; Ur III)
nu₂-a-zu-ne                         'when you lie down'   Lugalbanda I 235 (OB)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §28.6 exx. 241–254)

Zólyomi has a lovely example of the imperfective meaning 'whenever':

```
{d}lamma tar-sir₂-sir₂-ra kisal {d}ba-u₂-ka ku₄-ku₄-da-ne₂ … mu-na-ni-ru-gu₂
'Whenever the protective spirit of Tarsirsir enters the courtyard of Bau, this statue will attract
 my lady's attention towards him.'
Nam-mahne 6 2:2–5 (L; Lag II) — Zólyomi 2017: §7.5 ex. 128
```

Three things to note. The **possessive is obligatory**, even when the subject is named
(_ka₅-a ti-la-ne₂_). It marks **A and S alike** — a "nominative" pattern, unlike everything else
in the language (Michalowski 1980). And the 1st and 2nd person forms end in the fossil case
**=ne**, which survives nowhere else — the _Locative 2_ of the _[noun cases](#)_ page.

### Other 'while' and 'as long as' clauses (rare)

```
geme₂-shul-e ur-{d}lama₃ in-tuku-a-ka   dumu nu-du₂-da
[…in-tuku-'a]=ak='a      ← genitive + locative: 'in that of …'
'that while Gemeshul was married to Ur-Lama, she did not give birth to any children'
NG 26 16 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.8 ex. 183

du-du a-na ba-ti-la-da   e₂-be₂ ur-e₂-ninnu dumu du-du-ke₄ in-gid₂
[a-na ba-til-'a]=da      ← 'with whatever (time) he lives'
'As long as Dudu lived, Ur-Eninnu, Dudu's son, … this house plot.'
NG 99 6–7 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.8 ex. 182
```

### 'As soon as' — modal _ha-_ + -a-ka

A construction Poebel thought was **concessive** ('although'). Jagersma, on the strength of newer
attestations, tentatively proposes '**as soon as**' (2010: §27.6.8). It becomes a set phrase in
OB literature:

```
ur₅-gen₇ hu-mu-na-ab-be₂-a-ka
'as soon as he had thus spoken to him'
Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta 347 (OB) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.8 ex. 184
```

## 12. The prefix {u}: 'having done X'

This is the third formal type, and it is extremely common. A **finite perfective** verb with the
prefix **u₃-** (Jagersma: _relative-past_; Zólyomi: _prefix of anteriority_) is subordinate to the
**following** verb. No -a, no case marker. It means "under the circumstance that X has
happened" — anterior **and** circumstantial (Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2; Zólyomi 2017: §9.4).

```
lu₂ dam u₃-taka₄   kug giĝ₄ 5-am₆ ensi₂-ke₄ ba-de₆
'When someone divorced his wife, the ruler took five shekels of silver for himself.'
Uru-inimgina 6 2:15'–18' (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2 ex. 22; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 182
```

It is **relative**: past relative to the next verb. With a future main verb it means 'future
perfect'; with a past one, 'pluperfect':

```
FUTURE:  {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄ sa-shu₄-gal-ne₂ u₃-ni-shu₄  shu mah ĝiri₃ mah-ne₂ an-ta he₂-ĝa₂-ĝa₂
         'May Ningirsu, having thrown his large battle net over him, set his mighty hand and
          foot on him from above!'
         En-metena 28 6:21–25 (L; ED IIIb)

PAST:    RU-lugal-ke₄ saĝ a-shag₄-ga-na-ka pu₂ i₃-ni-du₃  igi-nu-du₈ ba-dab₅
         'When a subordinate had built a well in the front part of his field, he seized himself
          a slave.'
         Uru-inimgina 4 7:17–21 (L; ED IIIb)
```

Two can be chained:

```
lu₂ e₂ a-ba-sumun u₃-un-du₃ …
'the person who, after the temple has become old, rebuilds it …'
Amar-Suena 12 32–37 (Ur; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2 ex. 23; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 185

u₂-gu a-ba-de₂ a-ba-ush₂  ur-{d}suen-ke₄ ur-{d}shul-pa-e₃ inim nu-un-ĝa₂-ĝa₂
'When she (a slave) gets lost or when she dies, Ur-Suen will not place a claim on Ur-Shulpae.'
FAOS 17:94 8–11 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2 ex. 26
```

### The letter formula

The most frequent {u}-form of all opens every Ur III letter:

```
lugal-e na-ab-be₂-a   ur-{d}li₉-si₄-na-ra   u₃-na-a-du₁₁
'Say to Ur-Lisina what the king says:'
   (lit. 'what the king says — when you have said it to Ur-Lisina …')
TCS 1:1 1–4 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2 ex. 39
```

Earlier letters use a plain imperative _du₁₁-ga-na_. Jagersma suggests the {u}-form is a polite
way of giving the same instruction.

### {u}: when, or if?

'Under the circumstance that' sits halfway between _when_ and _if_, and translations vary:

```
lu₂-sha-lim-e nam-erim₂-be₂ un-ku₅  ur-{d}ur₃-bar-tab-ke₄ ib₂-su-su
'When Awilum-shalim takes an oath about this, Ur-Urbartab will replace this.'
NG 215 32–33 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2 ex. 32
```

But Jagersma is firm that a {u}-clause is **not** a conditional clause (Zólyomi translates some
of them with 'if' — see §19): sentences exist that
contain both a {u}-clause **and** a _tukum-be₂_ clause, side by side (2010: §24.2.2 exx. 35–37):

```
im-ba igi u₃-bar   tukum-be₂ nu-ub-sar   ur-me-me-ke₄ ib₂-su-su
'When the tablet about this is looked at, if this is not written on it, Ur-Meme will replace it.'
NG 209 18–21 (N; Ur III)
```

## 13. Reason ('because')

Five constructions, and they **replaced each other over time** — useful for dating a text
(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5).

### ED IIIb: _bar_ 'outside'

'On the outside of' came to mean 'because of', just as English _about_ comes from Old English
_ābūtan_ 'on the outside':

```
bar she-be₂ nu-da-su₃-su₃-da-ka
[bar [she=be nu-i-n-da-su~su-ed-'a]=ak]='a
'because this barley could not be repaid by him'
En-metena 28 2:27 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5 ex. 138; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 106
```

Non-finite: _bar she lugal-TUKUL zuh-a-ka_ 'because of having stolen Lugaltukul's barley'
(RTC 96 5:10–11; L; OAkk).

### Ur III onward: _mu_ 'name'

'For the name of' — the **exact** equivalent of Akkadian _aššum_ 'because', from _ana šum_
'for the name of':

```
mu i₃-tur-ra-she₃   a₂ en-te-na-ka-ne₂ i₃-zi
[mu [i-tur-'a]=ak]=she
'Because he was (too) young, his winter labour is registered as expended.'
SNAT 325 obv 3–4 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5 ex. 141

mu nu-da-su-su-da-she₃         'because it cannot be repaid by him'   PIOL 19:344 3 (U; Ur III)
mu ur-mes-e sa-gaz in-na-ak-she₃ 'because Urmes had robbed him'      NG 42 5 (L; Ur III)
```

Compare the first with the ED IIIb _bar … -ka_ example above: same clause, two eras, two
heads.

### _-a-ke₄-esh_: genitive + adverbiative

ED IIIb Sumerian had a construction with suffixed /akanam/, preserved in the personal name
**ĝa₂-ka-nam-he₂-ti** 'May she live because of me!' By Lag II (Gudea's time) it had been replaced by
/akesh/ — and the **same name** reappears in Ur III texts as **ĝa₂-ke₄-esh-he₂-ti**
(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5, citing Krecher 1993c: 193):

```
kishib ur-esh₃-kug-ga nu-u₃-ĝal₂-la-ke₄-esh   ĝiri₃-ne₂ shu ba-an-shi-ti
[…nu-i-ĝal-'a]=ak=esh
'Because Ur-Eskuga's sealed document was not there, Girine received it from him.'
JCS 54 p. 2:4 rev 1–3 (N; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5 ex. 149

ur-saĝ ug₅-ga i₃-me-sha-ke₄-esh
'because they were killed heroes'
Gudea Cyl. A 26:15 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5 ex. 148

anshe zuh-a-ke₄-esh            'because of having stolen a donkey'  (non-finite)
                               NG 202 3 (U; Ur III)
```

One text even blends _mu_ and _-ke₄-esh_: _mu lu₂-IM-ka ba-an-ku-re-esh-sha-ke₄-esh_ 'because
they were turned into liars' (Pettinato _L'uomo_ p. 101:45).

### The comitative -da

Comitative 'with' sometimes means 'because of'. Jagersma knows exactly three finite instances:

```
e₂-an-na-tum₂ ensi₂ lagash{ki}-ra {d}inana-ke₄ ki an-na-aĝ₂-ĝa₂-da
nam-ensi₂ lagash{ki}-ta nam-lugal kish{ki} mu-na-ta-shum₂
'Because Inanna loves E-ana-tum, the ruler of Lagash, she gave him the kingship of Kish
 together with the rulership over Lagash.'
E-ana-tum 2 5:23–6:5 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5 ex. 151

u₂-a nu-zuh-a-da   u₂-a id₂-da an-e₁₁
'Because U'a had not stolen it, U'a went down into the divine river (for an ordeal).'
SRU 99 9:4–8 (N; OAkk) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.5 ex. 152
```

### The equative -gen₇ (possibly)

A sixth type may exist: a nominalised clause in the equative with causal meaning. Jagersma calls
his best example "close" rather than certain (§27.6.7):

```
nu-bar-re nam-nu-bar-ra-na ba-ni-de₆-a-gen₇
'as (= because?) the nubar-priestess brings him along in her position of nubar-priestess'
Studies Borger p. 73 15 (Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.7 ex. 176
```

### A possible concessive ('although')

Neither of our two reference grammars recognises a dedicated concessive construction. Foxvog,
however, states that copular subordinate clauses formed with the nominalising _-a_ "are often
used to convey the notion 'although, even though'" (Foxvog 2011: 49). His examples are plain
_-a_ forms of the copula, mostly negated _nu-me-a_:

```
lu₂ igi-na sukkal nu-me-a
'The person in front of her, though he was not a minister, …'
Inana's Descent 291 (OB) — Foxvog 2011: 49

uĝ₃-be₂ shika ku₅-da nu-me-a bar-ba ba-e-si
'Its people, though they were not broken potsherds, filled its outskirts.'
Lament for Ur 211 (OB) — Foxvog 2011: 49
```

Jagersma reads the same kind of clause as a temporal clause in the locative — including the one
ED IIIb example Foxvog cites (see §19). Note that _-a-ke₄-esh_ is **not** concessive: Foxvog
translates it 'because', exactly as Jagersma does.

## 14. Condition ('if')

Jagersma's corpus contains close to two hundred conditional clauses — nearly all from Ur III legal
texts, nearly all about the **future**, and **none unreal** ('if I were …'). How Sumerian
expressed unreal conditions is simply unknown (2010: §27.6.6).

### _u₄-da_: Lagash, ED IIIb to Ur III

The oldest: it is the only conditional construction already attested in ED IIIb, and it
continues into Ur III — but only ever in texts from Lagash, so it may be dialectal (fewer than a
dozen attestations; Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6). Literally 'in the time (that)' — the same
'when' → 'if' path as German _wenn_. Takes the **imperfective**:

```
u₄-da mu-bala-e   sa-shu₄-gal {d}utu … umma{ki}-a an-ta he₂-shu₄
'If I cross it, may Utu's great battle-net cover Umma from above!'
E-ana-tum 1 rev 1:24–30 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 154

u₄-da nu-she₃-sa₁₀-sa₁₀   ugula lipish-be₂ na-na-tag-ge
'If he does not buy it from him, the foreman should not let his anger about this touch him!'
Uru-inimgina 4 11:29–31 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 155
```

A rare perfective, because the condition is a state: _u₄-da udu e-bar₆ … kug giĝ₄ 5-am₆
e-ĝa₂-ĝa₂-ne_ 'If the sheep were white, they used to pay five shekels of silver' (Uru-inimgina 6
1:18'–21'). And _u₄-da_ need not be first: _ur-{d}ishtaran nu-banda₃ u₄-da shag₄-ĝa₂ he₂-dab₅
ib₂-be₂ …_ 'If Ur-Ishtaran, the overseer, says "May I keep what I want" …' (TCS 1:157; L; Ur III).

### _tukum-be₂_: the usual 'if' from Lag II onward

Attested everywhere, but only from Lag II (Gudea's time) onward — probably a recent innovation. Takes the
**perfective**, followed by an imperfective or modal main clause:

```
tukum-be₂ kug nu-la₂   ab-tab-be₂
'If he does not pay the silver, he will double it.'
NRVN 1:104 9–10 (N; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.3.2 ex. 7

tukum-be₂ nu-na-an-shum₂   e₂-a-ne₂-ta ib₂-su-su
'If he does not give it to him, he will replace it from his estate.'
TCS 1:177 8–11 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 159

tukum-be₂ la-ba-shum₂   tab-dam
'If this (silver) is not given, it is to be doubled.'
NRVN 1:116 8 (N; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 160
```

Its earliest attestation is on a Gudea statue:

```
tukum-be₂ mu-be₂ shu ur₃-de₃ ĝeshtug₂ he₂-em-shi-gub
'if (his) mind is fixed on erasing this inscription'
   (lit. 'if the ear should stand to sweeping the hand over this name')
Gudea Statue B 9:12–14 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 158
```

A few refer to the **past**, still perfective:

```
tukum-be₂ eĝer₅ ab-ba-na-ta-am₃ lu₂-sha-lim-e saĝ shuku-ra ba-ra-an-kid₇   lu₂-sha-lim sher₇-da-am₃
'If, after his father's death, Lu-shalim pinched off the best part of the subsistence field,
 Lu-shalim is at fault.'
NG 215 11–13 (U; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 162
```

Like _u₄-da_, it can come after other material: _geme₂-{d}dumu-zi-da tukum-be₂ geme₂ ba-la-la-kam_
'if Geme-Dumuzida is the slave woman of Balala' (AuOr 17/18 p. 228:40).

The etymology is probably _tukun_ 'immediately' + _-be₂_ 'its' + directive _-e_
(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6).

### The doubled verb: 'if he does …, if he does not …'

A legal formula: the same verb twice, positive then negative, with the first main clause left
out (Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6, following Falkenstein 1956: II 224):

```
su-su-dam   in-su nu-su   ib₂-tab-be₂
'It is to be repaid. If he repays it, <all is well>. But if he does not repay it, he will
 double it.'
UET 3:37 6–8 (Ur; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 166

tukum-be₂ u₄ 3-kam-ka {ĝesh}gu-za mu-de₆ nu-mu-de₆ …
'If I bring the chair on the third day, <all is well>. But if I do not bring it, (I will pay a
 fine).'
NG 131 9–15 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.6 ex. 170
```

### The modal _ha-_: 'should he …'

A verb with modal _ha-_ can be conditional, like English _should he arrive_
(Jagersma 2010: §25.4.2):

```
na-lu₂ dumu ur-sa₆-ga-ke₄ kug-ĝu₁₀ ha-ma-shum₂-mu   e₂-zu tum₂-ba
'If (= should) Nalu, the son of Ursaga, give me my silver, (you,) take away your house!'
NG 179 9–10 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §25.4.2 ex. 81

lu₂ umma{ki} he₂ lu₂ kur-ra he₂   {d}en-lil₂-le he₂-ha-lam-me
'Whether he is someone from Umma or someone from the mountain land, may Enlil annihilate him!'
En-metena 28 6:17–20 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §25.4.2 ex. 83
```

That last one — _X he₂, Y he₂_ 'be it X, be it Y' — is how Sumerian says **whether … or**.

### Aspect in conditionals: a summary

| Conjunction                      | Verb in the _if_-clause                  |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| _u₄-da_ (Lagash, ED IIIb–Ur III) | **imperfective** (like temporal clauses) |
| _tukum-be₂_ (Lag II onward)      | **perfective**                           |
| doubled verb                     | **perfective**                           |
| {u}-prefix                       | **perfective** (obligatory)              |

## 15. Purpose ('in order to')

**Always non-finite** (Jagersma 2010: §27.6.1). The recipe never varies:

> **imperfective participle + directive -e**, written **-de₃**

```
e₂ {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ka du₃-de₃   gu₃-de₂-a iri-ne₂ ĝir₂-su{ki}-she₃ gu₂ mu-na-si-si
[e₂ ninĝirsuk=ak du-ed]=e
'To build the temple of Ningirsu, (Magan and Meluhha) gathered for Gudea at his city Girsu.'
Gudea Cyl. A 15:9–10 (L; Lag II) — Zólyomi 2017: §7.4 ex. 122; Jagersma 2010: §28.1 ex. 4
```

The purpose clause's subject can be the **same** as the main clause's, or **different**
(Zólyomi 2017: §7.4):

```
SAME SUBJECT:
lu₂-zah₃ dab₅-de₃ ĝen-na              'who went to arrest a fugitive'
                                       DTCR 152 2 (L; Ur III)
gu₄ id₂-de₃ bala-e-de₃ ĝen-na         'who went to bring oxen across the river'
                                       TCTI 1:641 23 (L; Ur III)
a-shag₄ tum₃-de₃ am₆-ta-bala-e-da     'who crosses (the levees) to take away fields'
                                       En-metena 1 6:15–16 (L; ED IIIb)

DIFFERENT SUBJECT:
ku₆ hab₂-she₃ ak-de₃  amar-girid₂ muhaldim-ra  en-ig-gal nu-banda₃ e-na-shum₂
'En-iggal, the overseer, gave fish to Amar-Girid, the cook, so that he would make them into
 fish-oil.'
DP 307 obv. 2:1–6 (L; ED IIIb) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 124

ur-{d}hal-mu-sha₄-ke₄ lugal-ushur₄ simug-ra ha-zi-in dim₂-e-de₃  1 ⅔ ma-na zabar in-na-an-shum₂
'Ur-Halmusha gave Lugal-ushur, the smith, 1⅔ minas of bronze, to produce an axe.'
SNAT 535 1–5 (U; Ur III) — Zólyomi 2017: ex. 125
```

Administrative tablets use it constantly to label the purpose of goods (Jagersma 2010: §28.4.4):

```
4 {kush}a-ĝa₂-la₂ mun-gazi si-gi₄-de₃    'four bags for putting in spices'
                                         TPTS 1:135 1–2 (U; Ur III)
0.4.0 dabin esir₂-ra sa₁₀-sa₁₀-de₃      '240 litres of barley flour for buying bitumen'
                                         MVN 16:1257 obv 1 (U; Ur III)
```

Zólyomi points out the English parallel: the morpheme marking the infinitive is the same as the
one marking the indirect object — English _to do_ and _give it to him_ (2017: §7.4). The verb may
even cross-reference the purpose clause with a dative prefix: _u₃-shub mu-dub₂ sheg₁₂ had₂-de₃
ba-shub_ 'He shook the brick mould and left the brick to dry' (Gudea Cyl. A 19:3).

**Not every _-de₃_ is a purpose clause.** An attributive participle can be followed by the
directive case of its **head noun**. Compare (Jagersma 2010: §28.4.4 exx. 237a–b):

```
1 gu₄ … niĝ₂ gu₇-de₃ ba-de₆    'PN took one bull for food (lit. "for a thing to be eaten")'
zid₃ niĝ₂ gu₇-da               'flour for food (lit. "of a thing to be eaten")'
```

### A finite alternative: modal _ha-_ 'so that'

A clause with _ha-_ following a main clause can express purpose (Jagersma 2010: §25.4.2):

```
ga-na-ab-du₁₁   inim-ba ha-mu-da-gub
'Let me tell it to her, so that she may stand by me in this matter!'
Gudea Cyl. A 1:24–25 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §25.4.2 ex. 84
```

## 16. Comparison ('like', 'just as', 'as if')

A nominalised clause in the **equative** -gen₇. Comparison is the original meaning
(Jagersma 2010: §27.6.7):

```
niĝ₂ e₂-ninnu … mu-na-du₃-a-gen₇   u₄ {d}ba-u₂ nin-a-ne₂ e₂-sila-sir₂-sir₂ … mu-na-du₃-a   iri mu-kug
'Just as (when) he built the Eninnu for Ningirsu, he purified the city when he built the
 Esilasirsir for his lady Bau.'
Gudea Statue E 2:13 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.7 ex. 172

la₂-NI 330 she gur ur-mes-e e₂-kishib-ba-ka in-tuku-a-gen₇-na-am₃
'It was as if Urmes had a shortage of 330 gur of barley in the storeroom.'
NG 205 49–50 (L; Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.7 ex. 173
```

Like English _as_, German _als_ and Akkadian _kīma_, the comparative extended to **time**:

```
u₄ alan {d}shul-gi-e in-gub-ba-gen₇-am₃   tum₂-dam
'This is to be brought when Shulgi sets up the statue.'
YOS 4:56 20–21 (Ur III) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.7 ex. 174
```

Foxvog lists the temporal readings the equative can take in subordinate clauses: 'just as', 'at
the same time as', 'as soon as' (Foxvog 2011: 44).

Non-finite: _lu₂ tur gibil-be₂ e₂ du₃-gen₇_ 'like a young man who is building a house from
scratch' (Gudea Cyl. A 19:22).

## 17. Subordinate copular clauses

The copula _me_ subordinates like any other verb — but always in its **independent** form,
never the enclitic (see the _[copula](#)_ page, §4):

```
arad₂ {d}shara₂ i₃-me-a            '(to swear) that he is a slave of the god Shara'
                                  NG 212 17 (U; Ur III)
mu tu-ra i₃-me-a-she₃            'because he was ill'
                                  TCTI 2:3342 10 (L; Ur III)
lu₂-{d}li₉-si₄ lu₂-gi-na-ab-tum-be₂ i₃-me-am₃
                                  'It was (the case) that Lu-Lisi was its guarantor.'
                                  NG 70 9' (L; Ur III)
ur-{d}nuska-da nu-me-a             'without (the permission of) Ur-Nuska'
                                  NATN 368 3–4 (N; Ur III)
```

(Jagersma 2010: §29.6; Zólyomi 2014: §2.2)

## 18. Aspect inside subordinate clauses

The perfective/imperfective rules are mostly the same as in main clauses (see the
_[Perfective and imperfective](#)_ page). A few points specific to subordination:

| Clause                     | Aspect                                                                       |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **when** + past event      | perfective                                                                   |
| **when** + future event    | **imperfective** — _even if anterior_ (_u₄ … ba-ug₇-e-da-a_ 'when she dies') |
| **after**                  | perfective in the corpus (later texts have imperfectives)                    |
| **until** with _en-na_     | usually imperfective + modal main clause                                     |
| **{u}-prefix**             | **perfective only**                                                          |
| **if** with _u₄-da_        | imperfective                                                                 |
| **if** with _tukum-be₂_    | **perfective**                                                               |
| **purpose**                | imperfective participle (always)                                             |
| **pronominal conjugation** | perfective = anterior; imperfective = simultaneous                           |

The odd one is temporal 'when' with a future: English uses a present (_when she dies_), and
Sumerian an imperfective, even though the dying precedes the sharing. Jagersma notes one
exception with a perfective, which behaves like a conditional (_u₄ nu-un-su-ga-a_ 'when he does
not repay this', BBVO 11 p. 280; 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 117).

## 19. Where the grammars disagree

Sumerian has no native speakers to ask. Everything on this page is reconstructed from the texts,
and two careful specialists reading the same texts do not always reach the same analysis. The
disagreements below are not mistakes on either side; they are what an honest picture of the
language looks like.

**This site follows Jagersma (2010)** as its reference grammar, so his analysis is the one used
throughout the page. Zólyomi's (2017) is set out next to it so you can recognise it when you meet
it in his textbook, in ETCSRI's glossing, or in the wider literature.

### 1. Is _u₄ … -a_ 'when' a relative clause?

The most frequent temporal construction in Ur III texts, _u₄ [clause]-a-a_ 'on the day when …',
looks exactly like a relative clause with _u₄_ 'day' as its head. English translates it with one:
_the day **when** he came_.

**Jagersma: no — it is an appositive content clause.** A relative clause always contains some
element that refers back to its head: a person prefix, a possessive, a dimensional prefix. In
_u₄ … -a_ clauses there is never such an element; the clause does not say anything _about_ the
day. Instead the clause and _u₄_ stand side by side as two descriptions of the same time, in
**apposition**\*. The proof is that when several such clauses are coordinated, each repeats the
locative — and repeating the case marker is exactly what appositions do in Sumerian
(2010: §27.6.2, cf. §5.3):

```
u₄ {d}nanshe nam-lugal lagash{ki}-sa mu-na-shum₂-ma-a   {d}nin-ĝir₂-su-ke₄ mu e-ne₂-pad₃-da-a
[u₄] [… mu-nna-n-shum-'a]='a                       [… i-nni-n-pad-'a]='a
                         ↑ locative                                  ↑ locative again
'on the day when Nanshe had given him the kingship over Lagash and Ningirsu had nominated him'
En-metena 26 13–18 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §27.6.2 ex. 110
```

The same reasoning applies to _eĝer₄_ 'back': for Jagersma, _eĝer₄ in-taka₄-a-ta_ 'after he had
divorced her' is a content clause in the **genitive** depending on _eĝer₄_ — literally 'from the
back **of** his having divorced her' — exactly parallel to _eĝer₄ gurum₂-ma-ta_ 'from the back
of the inventory', where the genitive is plain (2010: §27.6.3).

**Zólyomi: yes — it is a relative clause.** He treats _u₄_ and _eĝer₄_ as ordinary head nouns in
slot P1 of a noun phrase, with the clause in the modifier slot P2 and the case marker in P5 —
_ud [relative clause]=ʾa_ 'on the day when', _eĝer [relative clause]=ta_ 'after'. For him this
is the main way Sumerian expresses temporal subordination: "relative clauses play an important
role in the interclausal syntax of Sumerian as temporal subordination is usually expressed with
the construction P1noun P2[relative clause]=P5case-marker" (2017: §7.3).

**What changes in practice?** Nothing on the tablet: both analyses describe the same sequence of
signs and give the same translation. What differs is how the construction fits into the grammar
— whether Sumerian temporal clauses are a kind of relative clause or a kind of noun phrase in
apposition. The computational annotation of the CDLI follows Zólyomi's line and tags _u₄_ as the
head of a relative clause.

### 2. Is a {u}-clause ever conditional?

The same ED IIIb sentence, translated by each grammarian:

```
lu₂ dam u₃-taka₄   kug giĝ₄ 5-am₆   ensi₂-ke₄ ba-de₆

Jagersma: 'WHEN someone divorced his wife, the ruler took five shekels of silver for himself.'
          Uru-inimgina 6 2:15'–18' — Jagersma 2010: §24.2.2 ex. 22

Zólyomi:  'IF a man divorced (his) wife, the ruler received 5 shekels of silver for himself.'
          Iri-kagina 3 2:16'–18' (RIME 1.9.9.3) — Zólyomi 2017: §9.4 ex. 182
```

(The two grammars cite the text under different editions' numbering.)

**Jagersma** treats {u} as a _relative-past_ prefix: 'under the circumstance that X has
happened'. He accepts that this meaning "is nearly a meaning 'if'", but insists the clause "is
not a conditional clause", because sentences exist containing **both** a {u}-clause and a
_tukum-be₂_ clause, which would be redundant if the {u}-clause were itself conditional
(2010: §24.2.2).

**Zólyomi** calls the same morpheme the _prefix of anteriority_ and describes it purely in terms
of time — anteriority relative to the following clause — while freely translating with 'if' where
the context calls for it (2017: §9.4).

Here the two are closer than the translations suggest: both agree the prefix marks anteriority,
and both agree real conditional clauses use _tukum-be₂_ or _u₄-da_. They differ on how far an
English 'if' is a fair translation.

### 3. 'He spoke to me about building' or 'He ordered me to build'?

```
e₂-a-ne₂ du₃-da ma-an-du₁₁

Jagersma: 'He spoke to me about building his temple.'   (locative)
Zólyomi:  'He ordered me to build his temple.'          (locative2)
Gudea Cyl. A 4:20 (L; Lag II) — Jagersma 2010: §28.4.4 ex. 216; Zólyomi 2017: ex. 102
```

Both analyse an imperfective participle in a locative case. Jagersma reads the locative as
'about', the content of the speech; Zólyomi gives _dug₄_ the force of a command. The difference
is a difference of translation more than of grammar — but it shows how much a translation of
Sumerian depends on judgement.

### 4. The _-ed-a_ of the imperfective pronominal conjugation

In forms like _ku₄-ku₄-da-ne₂_ 'as he was entering', is the _-a_ a genuine nominalising suffix?

**Jagersma: yes.** The imperfective pronominal conjugation is built on an imperfective participle
**with** the nominalising suffix {'a} — the fourth participle, which occurs nowhere else
(2010: §28.5–28.6).

**Zólyomi: probably not originally.** He finds the _-a_ "unexpected" and suggests it is "a
secondary development on the analogy of the preterite forms", starting from a reanalysis of the
frequent third-person form _ku~ku-ed=ane=e_ as _ku~ku-ed-ʾa=ane=e_ (2017: §7.5).

### 5. 'When' or 'although'? Copular clauses in _-a_

This disagreement is between Jagersma and Foxvog rather than Zólyomi. Both grammarians cite the
same ED IIIb tablet:

```
e₂-ki saĝĝa e-me-a

Jagersma: '(Eki) WHEN he was administrator'
          CT 50:26 3:6 (L; ED IIIb) — Jagersma 2010: §29.6.4 ex. 192

Foxvog:   'Eki, THOUGH he used to be the temple administrator'
          CT 50, 26 3:5–6 (ED IIIb) — Foxvog 2011: 49
```

**Jagersma** treats a copular clause in _-a_ like any other nominalised clause: in the locative
it is temporal, 'when …'. Negated, it becomes the lexicalised _nu-me-a_ 'without (the
permission of)', literally 'when it is not together with' (2010: §29.6.4).

**Foxvog** states that such clauses "are often used to convey the notion 'although, even
though'", and translates his two OB examples with negated _nu-me-a_ as 'though he was not …',
'though they were not …' (2011: 49).

The two readings are not as far apart as they look: 'when he was administrator' and 'though he
was administrator' differ only in how the clause relates to what follows, which the grammar does
not mark — only the context does. Both grammarians agree that _-a-ke₄-esh_ means 'because':
Foxvog's own examples of it, including Gudea Cyl. A 26:15, are translated 'because'.

A similar case: Poebel (1923: 167) took the _ha- … -a-ka_ construction to be concessive
('although'); Jagersma rejects this as not fitting the more recently identified attestations and
tentatively proposes 'as soon as' (2010: §27.6.8).

### 6. Different names for the same things

Finally, many apparent disagreements are only terminology. When you read Zólyomi or ETCSRI after
this page, translate as follows:

| This page (Jagersma)                           | Zólyomi 2017                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| nominalising suffix {'a}                       | subordinator suffix (slot S15)     |
| present participle (_ĝar_)                     | tenseless non-finite form, TL      |
| past participle (_ĝar-ra_)                     | preterite non-finite form, PT      |
| imperfective participle (_ĝa₂-ĝa₂-de₃_)        | present-future non-finite form, PF |
| relative-past prefix {u}                       | prefix of anteriority (slot S1)    |
| directive case _-e_ on a purpose clause        | non-human dative =/e/              |
| Locative 2 =/ne/ in the pronominal conjugation | archaic locative, L4               |
| perfective / imperfective                      | preterite / present-future         |

## 20. How a subordinate clause is built: a walkthrough

This section takes the pieces of Parts One to Three and lays them out in the order a reader meets
them when analysing a sentence. It describes how Sumerian scribes built these clauses; it does not
prescribe anything. Where more than one construction was in use, the **Ur III** form — the best
attested — is given first, and forms from other periods follow with their labels.

**Step 1 — The underlying clause.** Every finite subordinate clause contains an ordinary finite
clause. Strip the nominaliser from an attested subordinate clause and you can see it:

> _ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-she₃ e-ĝen-na-a_ 'when the ruler came to the women's quarters' (DP 218 1:7–2:1;
> L; ED IIIb) contains the clause _ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-she₃ e-ĝen_ 'the ruler came to the women's
> quarters'. (That bare clause is an analytical step, not a quoted form.)

**Step 2 — Finite or non-finite.** The choice is not free, and the corpus shows clear tendencies
(§4, §15):

- **Purpose** clauses are **always non-finite**: imperfective participle + directive _-de₃_.
- A **specific event with its own participants** is usually expressed by a **finite** clause.
- A **generic, permanent property** ('one who …') is usually a **present participle**.
- In Ur III administrative texts, short **formulaic time expressions** are typically
  **non-finite** past participles: _iti 12 zal-la-a_ 'when the twelfth month had passed'.
  **Step 3 — The nominaliser.** A finite subordinate clause ends in _-a_, written as a CV-sign after
  a consonant (§3): _e-ĝen_ → \*e-ĝen-**na\***. Coordinated clauses share one _-a_; the verb _ak_
  usually has none.

**Step 4 — The job, and so the case or the head.**

| Meaning                                         | Ur III                                                                      | Other periods                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| relative clause                                 | clause **after** the head noun; the head's case marker **after** the clause | same in all periods                                                                                           |
| _that …_ after _zu_, _ge-en_, _dug₄_            | clause in the absolutive; direct speech is commoner                         | same                                                                                                          |
| _that …_ after _mu lugal_, _lu₂ inim-ma_, _dub_ | clause in the genitive, often fronted with _-be₂_ on the noun               | —                                                                                                             |
| _when_                                          | **u₄** + clause + locative: _u₄ … -a-a_                                     | **ED IIIb**: bare clause in the locative is the administrative norm; _u₄ … -a-a_ in royal inscriptions        |
| _after, since_                                  | clause + ablative **-ta**; _u₄ … -a-ta_; _eĝer₄ … -a-ta_                    | **ED IIIb**: _eĝer₄ … -a-ta_ already attested                                                                 |
| _until_                                         | **en-na** + imperfective; or _en-na … -a-she₃_                              | —                                                                                                             |
| _before_                                        | negated clause + terminative: _nu- … -a-she₃_                               | —                                                                                                             |
| _because_                                       | **mu** … **-a-she₃**; or **-a-ke₄-esh**                                     | **ED IIIb**: _bar … -a-ka_ and _/akanam/_; _-ke₄-esh_ from **Lag II**                                         |
| _if_                                            | **tukum-be₂** + perfective; doubled verb _V nu-V_                           | **ED IIIb–Ur III**, Lagash only: _u₄-da_ + imperfective; _tukum-be₂_ first attested **Lag II**                |
| _like, just as_                                 | equative **-gen₇**                                                          | temporal 'when' already attested in Ur III; wider temporal and causal readings in later texts (Römer 1980)    |
| _although_ (?)                                  | —                                                                           | copular clause in _-a_, esp. _nu-me-a_ 'though … not', per Foxvog; Jagersma reads these as temporal (see §19) |
| _having done_                                   | prefix **u₃-** on a perfective verb; no -a, no case                         | **ED IIIb** onward                                                                                            |
| _while_ (pronominal conjugation)                | participle + possessive + case                                              | 1st and 2nd person forms mostly **OB**                                                                        |
| _as soon as_                                    | —                                                                           | **_ha- … -a-ka_**, mainly **OB** literary                                                                     |

**Step 5 — Word order.** Subordinate clauses normally precede the main clause, and the main verb
comes last. The two attested forms of the same 'when' show the change between periods:

> **ED IIIb**: _ensi₂ e₂-mi₂-she₃ e-ĝen-na-a_ 'when the ruler came to the women's quarters'
> (DP 218 1:7–2:1; L)
>
> **Ur III**: _u₄ ur-kish{ki}-ta i₃-im-ĝen-na-a_ 'when he came from Urkish' (TCL 2:5565 3–4; D)

## Notes on terminology

Terms marked with an asterisk\* in the text, in the order they appear.

**determinative** — A sign written next to a word to tell the reader what category it belongs
to (a god, a place, a wooden object) and not pronounced. The same sign can also be an ordinary
word (_ĝesh_ 'tree', _ki_ 'place'): it is a determinative only when it is attached to another
word. Written in curly braces on this page, as in ATF.

**subordinator / nominalising suffix** — The suffix _-a_ (/'a/) at the very end of a finite verb
that turns its clause into a noun phrase. Zólyomi's term (subordinator, slot S15) and Jagersma's
(nominalising suffix {'a}) name the same morpheme.

**unoriented** — Said of a participle that does not have a fixed relation to its head noun.
English _hated_ always describes the thing hated; a Sumerian participle can describe the
doer, the thing done-to, the recipient, the place — whatever the context requires.

**restrictive / non-restrictive** — A restrictive relative clause picks out _which_ one (_the
farmers who protested were arrested_ — only those). A non-restrictive one merely adds information
(_the farmers, who protested, were arrested_ — all of them).

**complement clause** — A clause that serves as the subject or object of a verb, or the content
of a noun: _that she had run off_, _the oath that he would pay_.

**complementation strategy** — A construction that does the job of a complement clause without
being one: a purpose clause, a locative 'about', two main clauses side by side.

**pronominal conjugation** — A traditional misnomer for an archaic non-finite temporal clause:
participle + possessive pronoun (naming the subject) + case marker. _ti-la-ne₂_ 'while he was
alive'. Not a conjugation, and the pronoun is a possessive.

**Mesanepada construction** — A participle with its agent expressed in the ergative _before_ it,
named after the royal name _mesh₃-an-ne₂-pad₃-da_ 'young man chosen by An'.

**apposition** — Two noun phrases placed side by side to describe the same thing, each normally
carrying the same case marker: _Gudea, the ruler of Lagash_. Jagersma analyses _u₄ … -a_ 'when'
this way.
